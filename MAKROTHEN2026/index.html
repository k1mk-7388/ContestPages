This contest is scored differently, and it changes the plan

Makrothen has no multipliers at all. A QSO is worth the great-circle distance in kilometres between the two grid squares, times a band factor: 80m ×2.0, 40m ×1.5, 20/15/10m ×1.0. Two stations in the same square get a flat 100 points. The exchange is a 4-character grid locator and nothing else — no RST needed.

So there is nothing to chase and nothing to save for later. Every hour is simply worth the sum of the distances you can reach in it.

The band factor does not mean "work the low bands"

This is the finding that matters most, and it runs against the obvious reading of the rules. Across four years of this station's own logs (2022–2025, 4,191 QSOs), 80m produced just 6% of the score despite the ×2.0 — because from FN32 at night 80m RTTY averages only 2,942 km, so 2,942 × 2.0 is worth less per QSO than 15m at ×1.0 and 6,088 km. The factor does not overcome the distance deficit.

40m is the band the factor actually pays on — it is the one band that reaches Europe while still carrying a multiplier above 1.0, and it has the highest points-per-QSO in the contest. 15m carries the largest share of the score on volume and range together. 80m is a marginal band here, worth working when nothing else is open rather than as a strategy.

Where the points come from

Two grid fields — JO and JN, central and southern Europe — are 46.5% of four years of points between them. Europe as a whole is roughly 70%. That is the scoring engine, and it is a consequence of the field: the entrant list runs about 58% European against 29% North American, and from FN32 nearly every one of those stations is a 5,500–7,000 km contact.

The highest value per QSO is not Europe, though: PM (Japan) pays 11,102 points a contact against JO's 6,608. There are far fewer of them, but a Japanese opening on 15m or 20m is worth interrupting a European run for. At the other end, EM (the US southeast) averages 2,213 — the clearest example of a band-full of QSOs that barely moves the score.

The three periods cover the whole clock

Saturday 0000–0800Z, Saturday 1600–2400Z, Sunday 0800–1600Z. Their union is all 24 hours, so every UTC hour belongs to exactly one period and the band plan's 24-hour axis maps onto the contest exactly — this is why there is one day drawn rather than two.

The three are close in total score, but the overnight period is the most efficient: fewest QSOs, highest points each. That is the dark-path Europe and Asia window. The Saturday evening period is the volume period.

How this was built

  • An 83-day archive of spots to/from FN32 ± 1 grid square (111M spots, 14 Jul – 05 Oct 2026), reduced to opening and closing edges per grid field and band, measured as offsets from the terminator that bounds each path and resolved against the contest weekend's own sunrise and sunset. Fields rather than CQ zones, because the exchange is a grid square.
  • This station's own four logs, 2022–2025 — 4,191 QSOs carrying both grids, which is what makes the band, period and field splits above possible. The results tables carry none of that.
  • 6,373 entrant rows from eleven years of published results, for the participation mix and the avg-km/QSO comparison by continent.
  • The scoring model is validated, not assumed. Reconstructing each year's claimed score from the log, after excluding the duplicates each log check report names, lands within 0.004% on the cleanest year (2024: 6,061,064 against 6,060,832 claimed) and inside 0.4% on two more. 2022 is +1.3% and its claimed QSO count sits 11 below the log's own with no dupes listed to explain the difference.

Caveat, stated on the chart: the offsets come from 14 Jul – 05 Oct 2026; the window drifts one way through the season, 15 h 01 m of daylight down to 11 h 32 m. The target night runs 11 h 18 m, 0 h 14 m shorter than the shortest day in it. That is why the edges are measured against the terminator rather than in UTC — only the offsets carry across that gap, and the Carrington weighting already leans the sample toward the window’s recent end. This is an extrapolation rather than an interpolation.
