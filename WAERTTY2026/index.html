How the score is built

RTTY on 3.5/7/14/21/28 MHz. Unlike the CW and SSB weekends, everybody works everybody: there are no continental limits, and each station counts once per band. The exchange is RST plus a serial number.

  • Points: every QSO is 1 point, wherever it is. On top of that, every QTC — a report of an earlier QSO, sent or received — is 1 point to each side, but QTCs may only pass between different continents, at most 10 between any two stations, sent and received together.
  • Multipliers, per band: DXCC/WAE entities, plus the numbered call areas of W, VE, VK, ZL, ZS, JA, BY, PY and RA8/RA9/RA0 — so W1–W0 are ten multipliers on every band.
  • Band weighting: multipliers on 80m count ×4, on 40m ×3, and on 20, 15 and 10m ×2.
  • Score = (QSOs + QTCs) × the weighted multiplier total.
  • 36 of 48 hours for a single operator, in off-times of at least 60 minutes with no QSO or QTC logged.
  • No contest operation in 14099–14101 kHz.

QTCs are half the points

In an average year this station logged 1,305 QSOs and 1,474 QTCs: 53% of all points were QTCs. They come where Europe is loudest and most numerous — 10m and 15m carry most of them — so the high bands' modest multiplier yield is paid back in QTCs.

80m is the most valuable QSO on the air

An average year runs 2,779 points and 945 weighted multipliers, so one weighted multiplier adds as much score as about 2.9 QSOs. A QSO's real value is W + P·(band weight)·(its chance of being a new multiplier), and over three years of this station's logs that puts the bands in a clear order:

An independent check: what the skimmers heard

The Reverse Beacon Network's RTTY skimmers in W1, W2 and W3 — 5 to 7 of them each year, K1TTT's own among them in 2022 and 2023 — heard what this station could hear, whether or not anyone here was on the air. Over the four WAE RTTY weekends 2022–2025 they give, for every hour and band, the stations available and the multipliers among them. Calibrated against this station's own logs (it worked 0.16–0.28 of the stations heard per hour, at most 59 an hour across both radios, and sent 1.65 QTCs per European QSO), and played hour by hour under the real scoring with two radios — one signal at a time, sharing that hourly ceiling, each radio's new multipliers limited by its own QSOs, the pair of bands in each hour chosen for the largest score gain — they give this plan:

Off: Saturday 0710–1140Z, Sunday 0100–0400Z, Sunday 0700–1130Z.

Where the two sources agree, the answer is firm: Saturday and Sunday from 0700Z to past 1100Z are dead on every band — the skimmers hear almost nothing and the logs show almost nothing. That is nine of the twelve hours off. The other three fit best in the early-Sunday lull, 0100–0400Z, the quietest stretch in the logs outside the dead hours, where what is still on the air has mostly been worked.

Off-times need not start on the hour. The rule asks only that each last at least 60 minutes. Rerun in 10-minute steps — working rate and ceiling recalibrated from this station's logs at that resolution, block edges slid ten minutes at a time with 720 minutes off and no block shorter than 60 — the model ends both morning blocks about half an hour later, at 1140Z on Saturday and 1130Z on Sunday. That is worth 1.2% over the same schedule in clock hours, 1.7–1.8% in each of 2023–2025 and nothing in 2022. The search's own best schedule goes further, splitting the early-Sunday rest into single hours scattered across Sunday for another 1.3%; that is not taken, since the model counts the stations heard, not the run a station loses each time it goes quiet and comes back.

Keep the opening. Left to itself the hourly model would also drop Saturday 0000Z, but it sees only stations calling CQ, the ones to search and pounce, and not the run rate this station makes when everyone is fresh: the logs make Saturday 0000Z the single best hour of the contest. Even in the model's own terms, this schedule costs only 1.3% against the 10-minute optimum.

A third check: what the top northeastern single operators did

DARC publishes every submitted WAE log. The top five northeastern North American single-operator logs of each year 2022–2025, 20 logs from 11 stations (AA3B, AE1P, K1DC, K1LZ, K3MM, N1IXF, N2HMM, N3QE, VE3NZ, W3FIZ, W3LL), show where the stations that beat this one, or nearly did, actually spent their hours. In the eight clock hours 0700–1100Z of each day they were off the air in 86% of cases and logged only 2% of their QSOs and QTCs: those hours are dead for everyone. In the three early-Sunday off-hours they were off only 37% of the time. The twelve hours most of them sat out were Sat 0700–1200Z, Sun 0500–1200Z — one long Sunday-morning block rather than two breaks, which in the skimmer model costs 2.7% against its optimum, against 1.3% for the plan above, and came out ahead of the plan in 2025: within what this model can tell apart, so choose between them on how you want to sleep. Their two busiest bands match the plan's pair in 31 of the 48 hours. Where they differ, they kept 20m as the second band through the evening and into the first hours of Saturday, where the plan pairs 40m with 80m for its ×4 weight, and on Saturday afternoon ran 15m with 10m where the plan keeps 20m: they leaned toward rate, the plan toward multipliers.

Skimmers hear runners, not callers, so a multiplier that only answers is missed, and five to seven skimmers is a modest sample. The plan's value is in the comparison between hours and bands, not in its absolute score.

How this was built

  • An 83-day archive of spots to/from FN32 ± 1 grid square (127M spots, 22 Aug – 13 Nov 2026), reduced to opening and closing edges per CQ zone and band as offsets from the terminator that bounds each path, then resolved against the contest weekend's own sunrise and sunset.
  • This station's own three WAE RTTY logs, 2023–2025 — 3,916 deduplicated QSOs and 4,422 QTCs, with entity and call area resolved through the project's own callsign_lookup.

One limitation, stated plainly. The band plan groups openings by CQ zone, but WAE's multipliers are entities and call areas, which are finer. The chart tells you when a region opens; how many multipliers live there is a separate question it does not answer.

Caveat, stated on the chart: the offsets come from 22 Aug – 13 Nov 2026; the window drifts one way through the season, 13 h 35 m of daylight down to 9 h 50 m. The target night runs 9 h 48 m, 0 h 02 m shorter than the shortest day in it. That is why the edges are measured against the terminator rather than in UTC — only the offsets carry across that gap, and the Carrington weighting already leans the sample toward the window’s recent end. This is an extrapolation rather than an interpolation.
