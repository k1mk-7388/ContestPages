Makrothen Skimmer Anomaly

Eight skimmers that over-reported the caller, not the runner

On the Makrothen RTTY weekend, eight RBN skimmers reported the station answering on the frequency of a running station — on 42 to 50 percent of the spots where their output could be checked, against a 4.8 percent median across all skimmers. All eight run the same skimmer program, and every skimmer in the field that runs it is one of the eight. The same effect is present in their CW output at roughly a quarter the strength; because CW is spotted every day, it dates four nodes' changes to a staggered 2–6 October and confirms the behavior was still running on 15 October.

10–11 October 2026 · 195,068 RTTY spots · CW checked to 15 Oct · prepared by K1MK

This is an engineering notebook documenting my investigation into an anomaly in RBN spots seen during the 2026 Makrothen RTTY contest. The intent is to collect all the evidence and analysis in one place, so that the RBN community (in particular spot consumers like me) can understand what happened and how to mitigate for the upcoming JARL WW RTTY contest. The notebook is in roughly chronological order and is structured to provide context, methodology, findings, and conclusions, with supporting data visualizations and tables.

Where this started

Nothing about the investigation reported here began with a rate. It began with an N1MM bandmap that looked wrong.

Below is ten minutes of 40 m RTTY, on the evening of 10 October. The spots have already been through an operator's own cluster filter and a check for mis-decoded callsigns, so nothing here is raw noise.

Three stations were running: WT2P on 7061.8, VE2BVV on 7062.5 and 3V8LL on 7065.2. Each is followed down the map by a fan of other callsigns on its own frequency — ten entries on 7065.2 alone.

Of the ten on 7065.2, only one — 3V8LL — was the station running there. The other nine were answering 3V8LL, and every one of them ran somewhere else that weekend, so they are real stations and nothing about the callsigns is wrong. An operator working down the bandmap makes one contact and then hits nine more bandmap entries that are all the same running station.

Ten stations typically do not run on one frequency at the same time, so something was wrong. The rest of this report is the work to identify the cause. The short version: nine of those ten were answering 3V8LL rather than running, and a handful of skimmers were reporting them as though they were CQing on the frequency as well.

Callers being spotted on a runner's frequency is a well-known artifact that occurs at a low, manageable rate. This report measures that background at a few percent — a fact of life that assisted stations are accustomed to dealing with. What made the Makrothen weekend different was how much more often it occurred.

How a spot is judged

The core measurement (sections 1–7) is RTTY only: every spot whose mode is anything else — CW, FT8, PSK — is discarded before the first grouping, not filtered afterwards. That matters more than it sounds, because the beacon artifact found in the CW analysis (section 8) comes from the international beacon frequencies, which are CW, and cannot reach it.

Group every RTTY spot by band, frequency to 0.1 kHz, and a five-minute slot. Call each of those buckets a run frequency cell. Within each cell, the callsign that the most distinct skimmers report is taken as the station holding the frequency. Where at least four skimmers back that callsign, the run frequency cell is decided (assigned to a runner), and any spot in it naming a different station is counted as off-consensus.

Off-consensus is a measurement, not a verdict. A spot can be off-consensus for several unrelated reasons — a mis-decoded callsign, a station answering rather than running, a receiver whose frequency readout is offset, a report that arrives after the station has moved, a frequency changing hands inside the slot — and sections 6 and 7 separate them. Every skimmer in the field produces some: the controls run at 1.9 to 4.1 percent. The word mis-attribution is reserved here for the one mechanism this report is about — a correctly decoded callsign reported as though it held a frequency it was only answering on.

On this weekend 15,245 run frequency cells reached that threshold. Rates below are expressed over judged spots — those landing in a decided run frequency cell — not over a skimmer's total output.

The denominator decides the answer, so it is stated everywhere. Dividing instead by total spots understates any skimmer that rarely lands in a decided run frequency cell. In CQ WW RTTY, VU2OY-9 reads 5.6 percent that way (581 of 10,423) and 85.8 percent over judged spots (581 of 677). Judged is the right denominator for deciding whether to trust a skimmer, but it rests on a thin slice when a skimmer is seldom judged — VU2OY-9 is judged on 6 percent of its own output. Neither figure is meaningful without the count beside it.

1 · The split is not a gradient

A faulty decoder would be expected to spread across a range. These do not. Eight skimmers sit between 41.6 and 50.2 percent, the ninth-worst at 9.0 percent, and the bulk of the field near 4 percent. MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 differ by 2 points, and K1RA and K1RA-4 by 3, and that resemblance understates the real grouping. Five of the eight instances belong to two operators: K1RA, K1RA-4 and KW4VA are all run by Andy K1RA, and MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 are both run by Nathan M9PSY. The eight instances are therefore five operators — KC2DAC, K3FEF, MM0ZBH, and those two — and the operator, not the callsign, is the natural unit, because a decoder is configured per operator. (Section 8 shows the rollout was per instance, so the count of independent events lies between five and eight.)

They need not be co-sited, and the spot data says they probably are not: on identical band coverage the two K1RA instances overlap on only 23.4% of frequency-and-moment keys and the two M9PSY instances on 38.8%, against 0.8–19.5% for fifteen unrelated pairs the same weekend. Two skimmers at different locations genuinely hear different things. What binds them is the operator, not the antenna — so "eight at once" reads as five operators changed something, one of them across three instances.

2 · It is not one loud station

3V8LL, Tunisia, drew the weekend's largest pile-ups and was the first case noticed. It is not the cause. The eight skimmers go off-consensus on 68 to 74 percent of every runner they hear, against 25 to 34 percent for the reference skimmers, and no single runner accounts for more than 4 to 9 percent of any one skimmer's errors. The behavior follows the skimmer, not the running station.

3 · It does not worsen with the pile-up

This is the result that rules out a decoder simply being swamped. If a crowded frequency were overwhelming these skimmers, the rate should climb as the crowd grows. It does not move. The reference skimmers, meanwhile, get steadily better as more skimmers agree.

6 · What it is not

Not a frequency calibration error — for these eight

An offset readout would drop a receiver's spots into the wrong frequency bin, where the consensus names a different station, producing this signature with nothing wrong at the decoder. Comparing each receiver's reported frequency for the same station, band and slot against the median of the others, every skimmer ranked on this weekend — flagged and reference alike — returns a median offset of 0.000 kHz. The flagged skimmers do show 3.4 to 7.0 kHz of scatter against the reference skimmers' 1.4 to 2.2, which is what decoding a different signal looks like rather than a shifted dial.

That control was run on the Makrothen weekend, and one station needed it on CQ WW. Manoj VU2CPL, co-author of the Meridian skimmer software, reproduced this report's method from the RBN archives and pointed out that VU2OY-9 — CQ WW's 85.8 percent outlier — is a receiver frequency offset rather than caller mis-attribution. Re-running the same control on the CQ WW days confirms it:

40 m  +100 Hz  ·  20 m  +200 Hz  ·  15 m  +300 Hz  ·  mean +13.7 ppm

The offset scales with band, which is the signature of a fractional error in the sampling clock rather than anything in the decoder, and it is identical on CW and RTTY — +100 Hz on 40 m and +300 Hz on 15 m in both — which no decoder fault would produce in two unrelated demodulators. At +300 Hz on 15 m a 0.1 kHz consensus bin places nearly every spot in a neighboring cell, so this method scores the station as off-consensus by construction. It also explains the oddity noted below, that only 677 of that station's 10,423 spots were judgeable at all. VU2CPL reports the cause as a calibration bug applying another receiver's correction constant, now fixed.

The lesson for the method is general: a per-skimmer frequency check has to run before any off-consensus rate is believed, and it has to run on the same days as the rate. A clock error of a few parts per million is indistinguishable from caller mis-attribution in the output, and distinguishable immediately in the frequencies.

Not mis-decoded callsigns

An off-consensus spot can carry a garbled version of the runner's own call rather than somebody else's — a single-character RTTY error, published because the runner's CQ preceded it. That is a decoder fault, not a frequency-attribution fault, and it is a different problem with a different fix. Classifying every off-consensus spot by its edit distance from the cell's consensus holder separates the two, a test proposed by VU2CPL and run here on both weekends:

The headline was never carried by decoder errors.

Not separable by signal strength

SNR carries no usable information here. Spots matching the consensus have a median of 24 dB over 111,448 observations; those that do not, 25 dB over 24,064. An SNR floor makes matters slightly worse up to 30 dB and only reduces the caller share at 40 dB, where it also discards 88 percent of correct spots.

Not visible in the archive's other fields

The RBN daily archive has carried a stable 13 columns since 2016 and holds no validity or quality flag. Of the three quality-adjacent fields, two are inert for RTTY: speed is a constant 45 baud, and mode reads CQ on 117,504 of 117,521 RTTY spots on 10 October, because the aggregator tags RTTY that way regardless of what was sent.

7 · The common factor is one skimmer program

PSK Reporter publishes the decoder software each reporting station used over the preceding seven days, a window that covers this contest. Joining that list against the measured rates answers the question the spot data cannot: these skimmers have one thing in common, and it is not geography, antennas, or an operator.

Nine stations were listed as running UberSDR Skimmer when this was first checked on 14 October. Eight of them produced enough RTTY on the Makrothen weekend to be judged, and those eight are exactly the eight skimmers in section 1. The ninth, M9PSY, is Nathan's third callsign and posted no RTTY. The partition holds in both directions:

  • No false negatives. Every judged UberSDR Skimmer station is in the 41.6–50.2 percent group; the group's median is 44.3 percent.
  • No false positives. Every ranked skimmer above 30 percent runs UberSDR Skimmer. The worst skimmer on any other software is 21.5 percent, and the controls run unrelated programs — CW reporter, CWSL_DIGI, and VE3NEA's own RTTY Skimmer Server v.1.3.0.432.

A tenth, K1HTV-4, joined the roster on 15 October, after the measurements above. Its first full day of CW is covered in section 9; one day is too short a record to say whether it carries the defect. It is listed here because it runs the program, not because it has been measured.

UberSDR is an open-source, web-based HF SDR platform built on ka9q-radio (ubersdr.org). Its published skimming support is aimed at FT4, FT8 and WSPR, plus a driver for VE3NEA's Skim Server; a dedicated RTTY skimmer is not part of its documented feature set, which is consistent with RTTY decoding being recent. This report does not identify which part of the program is responsible. What the data supports is narrower and sufficient: the behavior tracks the program, not the site, the antenna, or the operator.

What this does not establish. The software list covers only the last seven days, so it describes what these stations run now, not what they ran during CQ WW RTTY three weeks earlier. It therefore cannot show whether K1RA and K1RA-4 — clean at 5.4 percent then — changed programs, changed versions, or changed a setting. The version string for UberSDR Skimmer is not published in that table either, so the eight cannot be confirmed to share one build.

A second grouping, and a different mechanism

The same join surfaced four stations running Meridian v0.1.25 — LB9KJ-1, VU24DX-1, VU2CPL-88 and VU2OY-9 — all reading above the field median. An earlier version of this report treated them as a milder case of the same effect. They are not, and the correction is owed to Manoj VU2CPL, co-author of Meridian, who reproduced the method independently and went through the off-consensus spots against his stations' own records.

Two separate things were happening, neither of them what this report described:

  • VU2OY-9's 85.8 percent is the frequency offset described in section 6, not mis-attribution at all.
  • The rest is mostly garbled decodes of the runner's own callsign — 39 to 57 percent of their off-consensus spots, against 0 to 1 percent for the UberSDR Skimmer group. The two programs show different faults, not one shared conceptual error, and the sentence in an earlier version suggesting otherwise was wrong.

On the caller mechanism itself the Meridian stations sit between the controls and the UberSDR Skimmer group: 2.9 and 4.2 percent of judged spots in CQ WW, 7.6 and 9.8 percent over the Makrothen weekend, against 1.9 to 4.1 percent for controls and 40.5 to 48.6 percent for the eight. VU2CPL's own figures for his skimmers are lower again, at the reference level, and the difference is mostly one of definition. Manoj splits the non-garble disputed spots in two — callers, which other skimmers also heard calling on that frequency, and unconfirmed calls, which only that skimmer reported there — and counts only the former as callers; this report counts both. The split can be rebuilt from the public archive alone: it reproduces his garble shares almost exactly and his caller and unconfirmed shares to within several points. On his definition the Meridian caller rate falls to 2.1 to 5.4 percent of judged spots, against 2.0 to 2.8 percent for controls, while the UberSDR Skimmer nodes stay at 30 to 34 percent. His stations' own logs added what the archive cannot show — the message context of each call — which showed that the garbles follow the runner's own CQ. Both measures put VU2CPL's skimmers far below the eight, which is the comparison that matters. Manoj reports the garble leak is being closed in the next Meridian release.

A third case: KM3T-5

One further station is worth the same treatment, and the suggestion is VU2CPL's. On this report's measure — any off-consensus spot that is not a garble — KM3T-5 reads 13.0 percent in CQ WW, above every Meridian station and the highest among skimmers reporting that weekend, and then 2.1 percent over Makrothen. OK2PMU's excess splits more evenly: of its 10.3 percent off-consensus rate, 4.6 points are garbles and 5.6 are callers. Neither station was examined in this report before VU2CPL pointed at them.

Over the CQ WW weekend KM3T-5 went off-consensus on 15.0 percent of its 29,062 judged spots: 13.0 points that are not garbles and 2.0 that are. Its garble rate is among the lowest in the field, but on VU2CPL's stricter definition only 2.5 points of the 13.0 are confirmed callers, which is the reference level. The rest are not callers at all. Measured against three regional skimmers within 400 km — WC2L, WZ7I and N2YCH — it also named the runner in only 87.5 percent of the cells it spotted into, against 98.4 percent over Makrothen, while they moved by under two points: a 9-point deficit specific to this skimmer and this weekend, on all three of its bands. (WC2L posted no RTTY on 26 September, so that day's hourly comparisons rest on the other two.)

What KM3T-5's spots actually were

Of KM3T-5's 3,785 disputed spots in CQ WW that are not garbles, only 19 percent name a caller that other skimmers also heard calling there. Half name a station that was itself running somewhere else at the time — against 0 to 3 percent for its peers, and 2 percent for KM3T-5 itself over Makrothen — and 45 percent of those are on a different band from the one the station was running on. Another 13 percent name a station no other skimmer reported at all, against about 1 percent for the peers. Nothing about the frequencies explains this: there is no offset, and no harmonic, mixing or image relation between where KM3T-5 placed a station and where it was.

Time does explain it. In 1,976 of these spots KM3T-5 put a station on the exact frequency that station had itself run on earlier — a median of 55 minutes earlier, and more than an hour in 772 cases — after someone else had taken it. Its three peers between them produced about a hundred such spots, typically five minutes old. The 654 stations involved are mostly widely heard DX stations, not local ones that might still be audible only in New Hampshire. The skimmer was reporting stations at frequencies they had left. Its CW output, decoded by the same node, shows the same fault at a milder stage. In the hours concerned its CW spots arrived 162 to 412 seconds behind the first skimmer to hear the same runner, against 7 to 228 seconds for the other skimmers in the comparison, and it made 171 stale CW spots to its peers' 0 to 16. A skimmer that has fallen behind reports a station late, and a late enough report names it on a frequency it has already left.

During CQ WW RTTY, which ran continuously from 0000Z on 26 September to 2400Z on the 27th, the fault kept much the same hours on both days. Measured on KM3T-5's RTTY output, it began at 1400Z on the 26th and was fully on from 1500Z until 2200Z (10 a.m. to 6 p.m. local, EDT); on the 27th it began an hour earlier, at 1300Z, was fully on from 1400Z, and was still on when the contest ended at 2400Z (8 p.m. local). Each time it started and stopped within an hour or so, and outside those hours KM3T-5 was as good as its peers. Its CW latency was elevated over about the same windows. 96 percent of the stale spots fall in those hours, where they make up 27.5 percent of everything KM3T-5 reported, against 0.4 percent in its other hours. In the worst hours nearly half of its output was stale, enough by itself to account for most of the deficit.

And the fault belonged to the CQ WW weekend alone. Over Makrothen, two weeks later, it was gone, or at least did not show itself: stale spots were 0.2 percent of KM3T-5's RTTY output, with no hour above 2 percent; it named the runner 98.4 percent of the time; and its CW spots were as prompt as anyone's. Nor does its runner-naming show anything like it on any other RTTY weekend in its 2025–26 record.

A second skimmer at the same site settles where the fault lies. KM3T-2 runs at the station owner's QTH alongside KM3T-5, on its own receiver and antenna, and decodes CW. It was clean in every hour of the weekend: its CW runner-naming share stayed within about five points of the regional peers' throughout, with 5 stale spots in 6,108. Weather, propagation and anything else common to the site reached both. What failed was KM3T-5's own chain — its antenna, receiver, or computer and software — and the form the failure took, latency in two modes at once, is the form a processing backlog takes.

Not the contest, not the weather

It was not the size of the contest. A year earlier, through a busier CQ WW — 2.25 million RTTY spots against 1.43 million — KM3T-5 matched its peers to within a point, and to within 1.3 points on every other 2025–26 RTTY weekend where it could be compared.

A nor'easter passed off the southern New England coast that weekend, and K1MK suggested it as the trigger — through precipitation static, lightning, or wind shaking hardware loose enough to arc. Each was tested against the hours in which the fault appeared:

  • Rain, from the four nearest working gauges (20 to 44 km): rain fell in 46 of the weekend's 47 hours, and the wettest hour, 0.30 inches, came with normal reception while other hours of 0.22 to 0.25 did not.
  • Wind, from the four nearest stations: of 24 hours with gusts of 30 to 45 knots, 10 were affected and 14 normal — including all eight with the strongest gusts. Gust strength and the deficit are uncorrelated.
  • Lightning nearby, from the surface network and the GOES-East satellite's lightning mapper: the first day's worst hours coincided with the storm's closest approach, about 100 km, but as much lightning earlier that day left reception normal; the second day's ten affected hours had almost no lightning within 600 km.
  • Lightning by skywave, from storms out past 6,000 km, matched hour for hour across the two geomagnetically quiet days: no band of distance followed the fault, and hours with comparable distant lightning gave opposite results.
  • The same site: KM3T-2, in the same weather, was unaffected.

Each pointed away from the weather without ruling out every form of it — noise picked up by KM3T-5's own antenna and not its neighbor's could still have flooded its decoder — so the investigation did not produce a clean answer. What triggered the fault is not established here. It lies in KM3T-5's own antenna, receiver or computer, and the host's own records for that weekend — processor load, the skimmer's logs, anything changed on that node since CQ WW 2025 — would settle it where the spot archive cannot.

8 · The same effect in CW, roughly a quarter as strong

These skimmers post CW as well as RTTY — 3,246 to 14,298 CW spots each over the Makrothen weekend — so the same test can ask how far the defect reaches. It reaches CW. Every one of the eight is elevated, and the excess appeared between the two weekends exactly as it did in RTTY, while the controls did not move.

Every CW figure here excludes the international beacon frequencies, and an earlier version of this report did not. The NCDXF/IARU beacon project puts eighteen beacons on one frequency per band — 14100.0, 18110.0, 21150.0, 24930.0 and 28200.0 kHz — each transmitting for ten seconds in rotation. A five-minute consensus slot can name only one holder, so every other beacon heard in that slot was scored as off-consensus while being entirely correct.

It was not a constant offset to subtract. It accounted for 65.3 percent of K3FEF's CW misses, 56.3 percent of MM0ZBH's and 55.6 percent of K1RA-4's, but 0.0 percent for four of the seven controls, which simply do not cover those frequencies. The published CW excesses were therefore inflated by roughly two to three times, and every number below is the corrected one. It was found by examining a newly-added node's first 23 spots by hand, which is an argument for reading individual spots and not only rates.

The RTTY analysis is unaffected, because the beacons are CW and the primary measurement discards every non-RTTY spot before it groups anything. Re-running sections 1–7 with these frequencies excluded moves the group median from 44.3 to 44.4 percent: 0.85 percent of all RTTY misses sit on an IBP frequency, at most 1.4 percent for any one node.

Not an artifact of the frequency bin

CW callers often offset a few hundred hertz to be heard, which could place a caller outside the runner's own 0.1 kHz bin and hide it from this test — the obvious reason a genuine CW problem might look mild. Re-running at ±0.2 and ±0.5 kHz tolerance rules that out. Widening the bin raises everyone's rate, because it also lumps genuinely separate stations together, but it shrinks the gap rather than opening it: the UberSDR-to-control ratio falls from 2.3× to 1.9× to 1.4×. There are no hidden offset callers to find, and the narrow bin is the more discriminating measurement. On the CQ WW weekend the ratio is 1.2×, 1.0× and 0.9× at the three widths — no excess at any of them.

Dating the change, node by node

RBN carries CW every day, so unlike RTTY it can date the change. Eighteen days were measured — 12 September for a baseline, then 26 September through 11 October — with each node compared to the same day's control median, so that a busy contest Saturday (3 October carried 947,000 CW spots against roughly 350,000 on a weekday) cannot be mistaken for a change in one skimmer. The controls held between 3.2 and 4.6 percent across the whole window with no trend.

Each node's own series was then split at the point that best separates it, weighting every day by its judged-spot count because a rate from 300 spots is far noisier than one from 11,000. There is no single changeover date. Four nodes transition cleanly, on four different days:

The two sharpest transitions are the best evidence for a released build rather than individual tinkering: K1RA-4 and MM0ZBH both step between 5 and 6 October, from +0.1 and +0.7 points to +4.2 and +2.8, on eleven clean days before and six elevated days after, with no overlap between the two segments. They belong to different operators. Excluding the beacon frequencies shrank these steps but did not move the dates: three of the four are unchanged and MM9PSY's moved one day earlier.

The four datable transitions fall in two pairs, two on 2–3 October and two on 5–6 October. That spread is what adoption of a new version looks like as operators update at their own pace, rather than a single flag flipping everywhere. The four undatable nodes fit the same shape: two of them (M9PSY-2, KW4VA) were off the air entirely for the fortnight before they appeared, and came up already affected, which is what a node started on an already-updated install would do.

The count of independent events lies between five and eight. Section 1 groups the eight instances under five operators, which would make five independent events. The dating shows that instances under one operator did not change together: Andy K1RA's K1RA-4 steps on 5–6 October while his KW4VA first appears on 10 October, and Nathan M9PSY's MM9PSY steps on 2–3 October while his M9PSY shows no step and his M9PSY-2 arrives on the 9th. The rollout is per instance, not per operator.

Binary search was the wrong instrument, and the record is kept here deliberately. Bisecting the group median converged on 2–3 October after four archive days. That boundary is an artifact: the median covers whichever nodes clear the judged floor each day, and K3FEF and M9PSY first appear on 3 October already elevated, which moves the median without any node changing — two of the four continuing nodes actually went down that day. Bisection also assumes one monotonic step, and there is none. The per-node scan of all eighteen days answered the question instead, for twelve extra archive days and about 41 MB.

What CW does not show

Two things that hold in RTTY fail here, and both matter.

  • The partition does not hold. In RTTY the eight occupied the entire top of the field and nothing else exceeded 21.5 percent. In CW they do not lead: OH2BBT at 52.1 percent, OK2RZ at 51.2, K5KHK at 34.0 and OK4YL at 30.5 are all worse, and none of them runs UberSDR Skimmer. CW has its own unrelated bad actors — K5KHK was also 41.1 percent on the earlier weekend — and the UberSDR group is merely elevated within a noisier field rather than defining its top.
  • The real-call test stops discriminating. In RTTY, 90 to 97 percent of the group's off-consensus calls were confirmed by four or more other skimmers against 80 to 91 percent for controls. In CW both sit at 77 to 89 percent. Everyone's CW misses are mostly real callers, so that test no longer separates the groups — it only confirms the mechanism is caller confusion in both.

The useful consequence is for localizing the fault: because CW is affected too, this is not purely a broken RTTY decoder. Something shared across modes changed. But the amplitudes differ by roughly fivefold, so a single shared cause and two separate ones fit the data equally well, and this report cannot choose between them.

They run +2.0 to +3.6 points above the controls, which sat at 3.4 percent over the same days. A tenth node, K1HTV-4, came on the air late on the 14th; on its one full day it ran +1.2, lower than any of the six and too short a record to rank.

Three nodes are currently dark

Three nodes report no CW at all over 12–15 October, and that needed checking rather than assuming, because a mode-filtered count cannot tell a skimmer that was switched off from one that is running and producing something else — a distinction this investigation got wrong once already. They are genuinely off the air: K1RA, KW4VA and M9PSY show zero spots in any mode on all four days. K1RA and KW4VA were both up on the 11th with near-identical output — 1,321 spots each, about 1,300 of them CW — and went silent together from the 12th; both are Andy K1RA's, while his K1RA-4 stayed up and is still the second-worst of the group. M9PSY has been dark since the 9th.

This lowers what an exclusion currently costs below the estimate that follows: three of the ten nodes on the list are not transmitting at all, so excluding them is a no-op while they stay down. They are still worth listing, because nothing suggests they were taken down to fix anything, and a node brought back up for a contest would come back on whatever it was running.

The cost is much smaller than the spot count suggests

On the Makrothen weekend these nine nodes produced 69,277 of 195,068 RTTY spots, so excluding them discards 35.5 percent of the stream — the figure that makes a blacklist look expensive. It is misleading, because most of what is discarded is either duplicated elsewhere or was never useful.

494 stations were reported only by these nine all weekend, a quarter of the 1,991 stations seen. But 456 of those 494 — 92.3 percent — never held a frequency anywhere, by the same four-skimmer consensus test: they were callers inside somebody else's pile-up, and a spot naming them was never workable. The genuine exclusive loss is 38 running stations, 5.5 percent of the 686 that ran. Of the spots that survive the exclusion, 97.8 percent name a station that ran somewhere.

Multiplier risk for this weekend: two entities

JARL WW RTTY counts DXCC entities plus the call areas of W, VE, JA and VK, per band, so the question is not whether a station disappears but whether a multiplier does. Re-running the Makrothen weekend without these nine:

  • No W or VE call area is lost on any band. All 15 areas visible from runners stay visible on 80, 40, 20, 15 and 10 m. The 38 exclusive runners include 21 W/VE stations spread over W1–W5, W7, W9, W0 and VE3, and every one of those areas is covered by another runner.
  • Two DXCC entities are lost: DU (Philippines) on 40 m through DU4WL, and GU (Guernsey) on 15 m through GU0UVH. 72 of the 74 entities visible from runners survive.

So the trade is roughly 20,000 misleading spots removed against two rare entity multipliers that would need finding by tuning instead. On that balance the exclusion is worth taking for an assisted entry, and the recommendation here is to apply it to all ten nodes — including M9PSY, which has no recent CW traffic to measure, and K1HTV-4, whose single day of CW is too short to rank, because both run the same program as the other eight. The cost figures above cover the nine nodes that were on the air during Makrothen; K1HTV-4 appeared afterward, so its cost is unmeasured.

These figures come from Makrothen, not from JARL — same nodes, same software state, four days earlier. Both are worked in every direction, which is what makes the proxy a reasonable one: JARL WW RTTY is a worldwide contest, and its multiplier structure treats JA, W, VE and VK symmetrically, each broken into call areas, rather than privileging any one of them. What differs is the scoring — Makrothen pays by distance with no multipliers at all, while JARL's multipliers are per band — so the two contests reward a different set of stations even when the same ones are audible. Expect the structure of the finding to carry over and the exact counts not to.

10 · Why AR-Cluster's skimmer flags pass these spots

CT1BOH's AR-Cluster documents SKIMVALID as being assigned "by looking back to see if there are two more spots of the same call, on the same frequency (± 0.3 kHz)". A caller inside a pile-up satisfies that by construction — Table 3 shows six skimmers reporting one caller at one frequency within seconds. The spot is classified Valid, and correctly so: the callsign is real and it did transmit there.

SKIMCQ cannot separate them either, being a flag for the literal string "CQ" in the comment field, which RTTY spots carry indiscriminately. Both flags behave exactly as specified. Neither asks whether the spotted station is the one holding the frequency, which is the only question that distinguishes a caller from a runner.

What this amounts to

The callsigns are correct. Between 90 and 97 percent of the off-consensus calls are independently confirmed by four or more other skimmers, and every one is a well-formed callsign. These skimmers decode accurately.

What is wrong is the frequency's meaning. A spot says "this station is workable here". During a pile-up that is true of the runner and false of everyone calling it. Every skimmer reports a caller occasionally; these eight did it on nearly half their checkable spots.

It is new. Two of the eight were measured two weeks earlier, in CQ WW RTTY, at 5.4 percent on roughly 32,000 checkable spots each. Three more were on the air that weekend producing 3,872 to 18,625 CW spots and not one RTTY spot; their CW decoders were working, and RTTY published nothing. In CW, four nodes' changes date to 2–6 October.

And it is fewer than eight independent events. Andy K1RA runs three of the eight instances and Nathan M9PSY two, so the number of independent changes behind this lies between five and eight; the dating in section 8 shows that instances under one operator did not change together.

The common factor is the software, and it partitions the field exactly. PSK Reporter listed nine stations running UberSDR Skimmer on 14 October, and a tenth, K1HTV-4, appeared the next day. Eight produced measurable RTTY on this weekend, and those eight are precisely the eight skimmers above. No skimmer running anything else exceeds 21.5 percent, and no control runs it.

One skimmer's CQ WW fault was something else. KM3T-5 fell behind: during the hours of 26 and 27 September described in section 7 it re-reported stations at frequencies they had left, in CW as well as RTTY. It is a different fault from the one above, it was absent at Makrothen and at the co-located KM3T-2, and what triggered it is not established.

11 · Scope and limits

  • Five operators, eight instances. Andy K1RA runs K1RA, K1RA-4 and KW4VA; Nathan M9PSY runs MM9PSY and M9PSY-2. Ownership comes from K1MK, not from the spot data, which cannot show it; so does the co-location of KM3T-2 with KM3T-5. It lowers the number of independent events below eight and raises the weight of each, but not as far as five: the dating in section 8 shows that instances under one operator did not change together.
  • "Absent" and "no RTTY" are different facts, and an RTTY-only spot count cannot tell them apart. Of the six not measurable in CQ WW RTTY, three (KC2DAC, MM9PSY, MM0ZBH) were in fact on the air spotting CW and published zero RTTY; only K3FEF, M9PSY-2 and KW4VA were absent entirely. An earlier draft of this report called all six "not on air", which was wrong and understated the case.
  • K1RA is the clearest evidence of a change, measured at 5.4 percent on ~32,000 judged spots in CQ WW and 45–48 percent two weeks later. In RTTY the change cannot be dated more precisely: it is contest-driven, there is almost none between those weekends, and Makrothen is the next RTTY contest after CQ WW. CW dates other nodes' changes in section 8, but not K1RA's.
  • CQ WW RTTY had outliers of its own, with different causes. VU2OY-9 reached 85.8 percent on 677 judged spots, a frequency offset (section 6), and CN3A at 24.7 and OK4ZW at 21.2 were also high. At a 1,000-judged floor the worst was KM3T-5 at 15.0 percent, 2.6× the median, a stale-reporting fault (section 7). What distinguishes Makrothen is that eight skimmers went wrong at once, each on a large sample, with one common factor; six of them still show 43–50 percent at the same 1,000-judged floor.
  • The weekends differ in size — 1,427,716 RTTY spots against 195,068 — so only the per-skimmer comparison carries weight, never the aggregate difference between contests.
  • The common factor is established; the defect is not. Shared software explains which skimmers misbehave, and the measurements in sections 2–6 constrain what kind of fault it is: flat in pile-up size, spread across nearly every runner, correct callsigns at correct frequencies. What the defect actually is, and which version introduced it, needs someone with the source — this report is intended to hand them a reproducible target.
