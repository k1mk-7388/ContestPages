    Investigation into an RBN Anomaly during the 2026 Makrothen RTTY Contest

    Eight Skimmers That Overreported the Caller, Not the Runner

    Abstract

    Over the three periods of the 2026 Makrothen RTTY contest (October 10–11), eight RBN skimmers reported stations answering a running station as if they held the frequency. Of their spots that could be checked against other skimmers' reports, 42 to 50 percent disagreed with the consensus. The other 30 skimmers with at least 100 such checkable spots, the threshold for ranking, disagreed with the consensus on a median of 4.2 percent of theirs. Only 43 skimmers worldwide sent RTTY spots to the RBN that weekend; the discrepant eight—nearly one in five of the 43 skimmers reporting—sent a third of all RTTY spots and 83 percent of the off-consensus ones. That was a historical departure, not a bad weekend: measured the same way over a decade of benchmark RTTY contests, the network's rate of reporting callers as runners had fallen from between 2 and 4 percent of judged spots in 2015 and 2016 to a median of 0.8 percent over 2023–2025, and what happened during the Makrothen contest put it above 10 percent for that weekend (Appendix D).

    All eight ran the same skimmer program, and every skimmer reporting to the RBN that ran it and qualified for ranking was one of the eight. A smaller excess of off-consensus spots is present in the CW output from the eight and was not corrected with the RTTY defect; whether the two have separate causes remains unconfirmed (§ 11). A fix for CW that appears targeted to correct a similar defect, released on October 27, has not reduced it so far (§ 11.3). Against a background of some 245 CW skimmers, the excess does not show in the network's global CW statistics at all, which have been nearly flat for 15 years (Appendix E).

    The defect was present by October 9. No skimmer running the program sent RTTY during the October 2 Weekly RTTY Test; in the October 9 test, the day before Makrothen, four were already reporting callers as runners (§ 15). A week later, during the JARL WW RTTY contest, five of the eight had been fixed in RTTY and a sixth, K1RA, returned to the air fixed on the second day of the contest; K1RA-4 still showed the defect until at least October 23 and was clean by October 30, and KW4VA stayed off the air (§ 15). The RTTY defect appears to have been resolved on every affected skimmer by October 30, with no recurrence in the Russian WW Multimode contest of October 31–November 1 or through the Weekly RTTY Test of November 6.

    Introduction

    Terminology. A runner calls CQ (a general invitation to make contact) on a frequency; a caller answers. A skimmer automatically decodes signals, interprets the decoded text, and sends spots, reports pairing a callsign with a frequency and time. Its decoder only turns signals into text; deciding which callsign is calling CQ and which is answering is interpretation, so decoder here means that first step and skimmer the whole process. A bandmap displays those spots by frequency. An assisted operator uses spotting assistance to locate stations. RTTY is radioteletype; CW (continuous wave) denotes Morse-code operation here; SDR means software-defined radio; and I/Q means in-phase and quadrature samples, which together represent a signal's amplitude and phase.

    This document began as an engineering notebook recording my investigation into an anomaly I encountered in the spots I received from the Reverse Beacon Network (RBN) during the 2026 Makrothen RTTY contest (October 10–11). The RBN is a network of skimmers that report spots to a central server, and its spots are used by many assisted operators in contests. I began looking into the anomaly right after the Makrothen contest, which was five days before the JARL WW RTTY contest, and this document details my inquiry into the defect as it progressed through the Makrothen contest and into the JARL follow-up. With the immediate episode over, this retrospective brings together the analysis and its lessons.

    This document follows the investigation in order: a bandmap that looked wrong (§ 1); a way of judging spots against each other (§ 2) and what it showed (§§ 3–9); a common thread, one skimmer program (§§ 10–12); the developer's acknowledgment and fix (§§ 13–14) and its test at JARL (§ 15); and what the episode says about testing software on a network other people depend on (§§ 16–17). Four other anomalies, with causes of their own, were identified in the course of this investigation and are noted separately in Appendix A. Appendix B gives the detail behind the CW excess of § 11: the checks that it is neither an artifact of the frequency bin nor a matter of contest load, and the dating of the change skimmer by skimmer. Appendix C looks at what the RTTY frequencies reported to 10 Hz show about frequency accuracy. Appendix D sets the episode against a decade of RTTY contests, and Appendix E against the CW record, where nothing comparable happened. Appendix F measures how many misattributed callers a busy CW band shows with no software defect involved, and Appendix G holds the search for additional groups of skimmers with a CW excess.

    Throughout what follows, a skimmer's rate is the share of its judged spots that are off-consensus, and a spot is judged when it falls in a 100 Hz frequency bin and five-minute time cell that four distinct skimmers attribute to one running station (§ 2).

    Here, the eight means the original eight high-rate skimmers in Table 1. The UberSDR Skimmer roster, or the roster for short, means every skimmer that PSK Reporter's software table (§ 10) has listed as running UberSDR Skimmer, or, from November 8, that the RBN's own node list shows running it (Appendix G.1): a skimmer joins it on the day it is first listed and stays on it afterward, so for any date the roster is the skimmers listed by then. Its members are skimmer callsigns, not operators. Through November 12 the node list added no skimmer that PSK Reporter had not already listed. Reference skimmers, also called the controls, means the seven comparison skimmers in Table 1; the RTTY checks of § 15 use the same set with PI4CC in place of SZ1A, and the mechanism split of § 8.2 uses the five controls named there.

    This was not a minor leak. It was a network-scale RTTY spotting failure during a contest, however inadvertent. Only 43 skimmers worldwide sent RTTY spots to the RBN over the Makrothen weekend, so with eight of them, nearly one in five, misbehaving so badly, the defect encompassed a large share of the network's RTTY traffic. Those eight sent a third of all RTTY spots on the RBN, 68,555 of 192,802, and nearly half of those that could be checked against other skimmers were off-consensus. Their 20,009 off-consensus spots were 83 percent of every off-consensus RTTY spot the network carried that weekend: the RBN was flooded with off-consensus spots, in the middle of a contest, delivered to assisted operators who were relying on an accurate spot stream. The defect came with new skimmer software and reached the live network without being caught first in testing, which is exactly what the testing best practices in § 17 exist to prevent. It also undid, in a single weekend, a decade of steady improvement in exactly this kind of error (Appendix D).

    Hanlon's razor applies: nothing here requires malice to explain it. Software marked as still in testing reached the live network during a contest; the law of unintended consequences took care of the rest.

    Nor is any of it a case against new skimmer software or the people who write it: the RBN needs both, and RTTY needs them most (§ 17). What this document criticizes is a risky practice, not people.

    1 · Where This Started

    This investigation did not originate with consensus spots, judged spots, or a rate; it began with an N1MM bandmap whose contents looked wrong.

    Figure 1 reproduces 10 minutes of 40 m RTTY on the evening of October 9 in North America (0040Z on October 10; times are UTC, marked Z), during the first Makrothen period. The spots had already passed through an operator's own cluster filter and a verification for misdecoded callsigns, so none of what appears here is unprocessed noise.

    Three stations were running: WT2P on 7061.8, VE2BVV on 7062.5, and 3V8LL on 7065.2. Each is followed down the display by a succession of other callsigns on its own frequency, 10 entries on 7065.2 alone.

    Of the 10 callsigns shown on 7065.2 in that 10-minute window, only 3V8LL was running there. The other nine were answering 3V8LL, and every one of those stations ran elsewhere at some point that weekend. All nine callsigns identify real stations; the error was their attribution, not the callsigns themselves. An operator working down the bandmap completes one contact and then encounters nine further entries, all of which lead back to the same running station. The bandmap offered 10 ways to work 3V8LL, but the log would accept only one of them.

    Ten stations do not ordinarily run on a single frequency simultaneously, so something was evidently amiss, and what follows documents my attempt to determine what it was. In summary, a small number of skimmers were reporting the nine callers as though they, too, were calling CQ on that frequency.

    Callers spotted on a runner's frequency are a familiar artifact. This investigation finds a background rate of a few percent of judged spots, which assisted operators generally accommodate. How many appear on a bandmap depends mostly on how busy the band is, so seeing more of them is not by itself a sign of a defect (§ 11.5). What distinguished Makrothen was how often it happened.

    2 · How a Spot Is Judged in This Analysis

    Unless a section states otherwise, the core measurements (§§ 3–10) are RTTY only: I discard every spot in any other mode before any grouping takes place, so the beacon artifact identified in the analysis of CW spots (§ 11), which originates on the CW-only international beacon frequencies, cannot contaminate the RTTY results.

    Makrothen is not a continuous contest. It runs in three eight-hour periods, Saturday 0000–0800Z and 1600–2400Z, and Sunday 0800–1600Z (rules), so every Makrothen RTTY figure counts only spots sent within those periods, and "the Makrothen weekend" means those 24 hours wherever RTTY is measured; the 2261 RTTY spots sent outside them, 1.2 percent of the two days, are left out. The CQ WW RTTY contest and JARL WW RTTY run 48 hours without a break and are taken whole. CW figures use whole days throughout, since none of these contests is a CW one.

    RTTY is confined to the five bands RTTY contests use: 80, 40, 20, 15, and 10 m. The few RTTY spots on other bands, 22 at CQ WW, 5 at Makrothen, and 50 at JARL, never reached a decided cell, so leaving them out changes spot totals and nothing else.

    I group every RTTY spot by band, by frequency to 100 Hz, and by five-minute slot, and I call each resulting bucket a run frequency cell. Since the RBN began accepting 10 Hz RTTY frequencies in March 2026 (RBN-OPS #9248), some skimmers report to 10 Hz and the rest to 100 Hz (the archive drops a trailing zero, so a skimmer's resolution has to be judged from all of its spots, not from any one; see § 20). Every frequency is rounded half-up to its 100 Hz cell, so 7042.85 always falls in the 7042.9 cell.

    Within each cell, I identify the leading callsign—the one reported by the largest number of distinct skimmers—as the candidate station holding the frequency. If callsigns tie, the one spotted first in the slot is used. A cell is decided only when at least four distinct skimmers report that leading callsign; it is then attributed to a running station. A spot in a decided cell that names a different station is off-consensus.

    Off-consensus is a measurement, not a verdict. A spot can be off-consensus for several unrelated reasons, among them a misdecoded callsign, a station answering rather than running, a skimmer whose frequency readout is offset, a report that arrives after the station has moved, or a frequency changing hands within the slot, and §§ 8–10 distinguish between them. Every skimmer reporting to the RBN produces some off-consensus spots: the reference skimmers I use as controls (Table 1) are off-consensus on 4.1 to 8.6 percent of their judged spots, with SZ1A the highest. I reserve the term misattribution for the one mechanism central to this investigation, in which a correctly decoded callsign is reported as though it held a frequency on which it was only answering.

    Over the three Makrothen periods, 15,012 run frequency cells reached the four-skimmer threshold. I express every rate over judged spots, those landing in a decided run frequency cell, rather than over a skimmer's total output, and I rank only those skimmers with at least 100 judged spots.

    The denominator decides the answer, so I fix it: every rate here is over judged spots unless a section says otherwise. Dividing instead by total spots systematically understates any skimmer that rarely lands in a decided run frequency cell: in CQ WW RTTY, VU2OY-9 registers 5.7 percent on that basis (594 of 10,423) but 86.3 percent over judged spots (594 of 688). Judged spots are the appropriate denominator for deciding whether to trust a skimmer, although the estimate rests on a narrow base when a skimmer is seldom judged, as VU2OY-9 is on only 7 percent of its own output, and neither figure is meaningful without the count beside it.

    3 · The Split Was Not a Gradient

    These rates do not form a continuous spread (Figure 2). Eight skimmers occupy a distinct high-rate group between 41.8 and 50.2 percent, and below them lies a gap of roughly 20 percentage points: the ninth-worst, HA8TKS, is at 21.6 percent, two Meridian skimmers follow at 19.2 and 17.9 (Appendix A explains all three), and the bulk of RTTY skimmers sits near 4 percent (Figure 3).

    Eight is not a small minority. Of the 43 skimmers that reported RTTY over the Makrothen weekend, 38 had at least 100 judged spots and qualified for ranking, and the eight constitute a fifth of them. They are also among the most prolific contributors, having sent 68,555 of the weekend's 192,802 RTTY spots, or 35.6 percent, and 20,009 of its 24,079 off-consensus spots, or 83 percent, so a spot consumer who applied no filtering received more than four in five of the weekend's off-consensus spots from these eight skimmers alone.

    MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 differ in rate by 2 percentage points, and K1RA and K1RA-4 by 3, but the relationship is closer than these figures suggest: five of the eight instances belong to two operators. K1RA, K1RA-4, and KW4VA are all run by Andy K1RA, and MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 are both run by Nathan M9PSY. The eight instances therefore belong to five operators—KC2DAC, K3FEF, MM0ZBH, and those two. The instances' differing transition dates indicate an instance-by-instance rollout: Appendix B.3 shows that instances under one operator changed on different days. Neither operator count nor transition timing establishes statistical independence.

    Instances under one operator need not be colocated, and the spot data cannot settle whether these were. Each operator's two instances cover the same bands; 23.4 percent of the two K1RA instances' reports coincide in callsign, frequency, and five-minute slot, and 38.8 percent of the two M9PSY instances' do, compared with 0.9 to 19.9 percent for 15 unrelated pairs of skimmers on the same weekend. But skimmers can hear different signals even at one site, so that overlap does not show whether either pair shared a receiving site. The common operator is known; how each operator's instances are set up is not.

    Two weeks earlier, over the CQ WW RTTY weekend, only two of the eight were active in RTTY, and both sat within the main cluster; the 58 ranked skimmers showed no comparable high-rate group (Figure 4). Nothing like the Makrothen split had appeared in a decade of RTTY contests, over which the network's rates had been falling steadily (Appendix D).

    4 · It Was Not Just One Loud Station

    3V8LL, in Tunisia, drew among the weekend's largest pileups and was the first case noticed, but it was not the cause. The eight skimmers make at least one off-consensus spot on 69 to 74 percent of the runners they hear, against 25 to 36 percent for the reference skimmers, while no individual runner accounts for more than 4 to 9 percent of any one skimmer's off-consensus spots. The problem therefore followed the skimmer, not any particular running station.

    5 · The Rate Did Not Rise with Consensus Support

    Simple decoder overload, a skimmer swamped by a busy pileup, does not fit: the eight's rate did not rise as more skimmers supported the runner (Figure 5). The number of supporting skimmers is an indirect proxy for activity, not a direct measure of pileup size. The reference skimmers, by contrast, improved as agreement increased.

    8 · What It Was Not

    8.1 · Not a Frequency Calibration Error—for These Eight

    A skimmer whose frequency readout is inaccurate would deposit its spots in the wrong cell, where the consensus names a different station. It would reproduce this signature without any defect in its decoder. I therefore compared each skimmer's reported frequency for the same station, band, and slot with the median of the other skimmers' reports. Every skimmer ranked on this weekend, flagged and reference alike, has a median offset of 0 Hz, except KW4VA and SZ1A at 100 Hz, whose small offsets § 9 measures. The flagged skimmers do show greater dispersion, a standard deviation of 3.4 to 6.6 kHz against 1.4 to 2.2 for the reference skimmers. That dispersion is characteristic of decoding a different signal, not of a displaced frequency calibration.

    I first ran that check only on the Makrothen weekend, but one skimmer needed it on CQ WW as well. Manoj VU2CPL, coauthor of the Meridian skimmer software, independently reproduced my method from the RBN archives. He pointed out that VU2OY-9, the 86.3 percent outlier of CQ WW, reflects a receiver frequency offset rather than caller misattribution, and repeating the same check on the CQ WW days confirms his diagnosis:

    40 m  +100 Hz  ·  20 m  +200 Hz  ·  15 m  +300 Hz  ·  +14.3 ppm on each

    The RBN's 100 Hz rounding exaggerates that figure. The more precise measurement described below places the error at +12.3 ppm, approximately 85, 175, and 260 Hz on those bands, each rounded up to the next 100 Hz. Because the offset increases with frequency, it indicates a small error in the receiver's sampling clock. Because it is identical on CW and RTTY (+100 Hz on 40 m and +300 Hz on 15 m in both), it cannot be a decoder defect, which two unrelated demodulators would not share. At +300 Hz on 15 m, a 100 Hz consensus bin assigns nearly every spot to a neighboring cell, so this method necessarily scores the station as off-consensus. This also explains why only 688 of the station's 10,423 spots could be judged. VU2CPL attributed the cause to a calibration defect, since corrected in the software, that applied another receiver's correction constant.

    To verify that diagnosis, and to examine every other skimmer, I applied the test described in § 9. Removing VU2OY-9's measured error of +12.3 ppm reduces its RTTY rate over the CQ WW RTTY weekend from approximately 86 percent to 10.3, on 7003 judged spots instead of fewer than 700. Over the JARL WW RTTY weekend, October 17 and 18, its error remained at +12.8 ppm in both CW and RTTY, indicating that the correction had not yet been deployed.

    VU2OY-9's clock was still about +13 ppm at Makrothen and JARL, yet it is absent from Figures 3 and 7: the error itself keeps its spots out of decided cells, leaving it only 9 judged RTTY spots at Makrothen and 30 at JARL, below the 100 needed to be plotted. At CQ WW its 10,423 spots still yielded 688 judged spots. With the error removed it would be 12.0 percent on 142 judged spots at Makrothen and 21.0 percent on 667 at JARL.

    Over the CQ WW weekend, CN3A at 24.2 percent and OK4ZW at 21.4 also have clock errors in RTTY, +12.1 and +6.1 ppm; with the errors removed, the two are at 18.5 and 19.3 percent, so neither error accounts for all of its skimmer's excess.

    The lesson is general: check each skimmer's frequency calibration before believing any off-consensus rate, and check it on the same days. A clock error of a few parts per million is indistinguishable from caller misattribution in the rates but becomes immediately apparent in the frequencies. § 11 shows the same test explaining three of the worst skimmers in CW.

    The result does not depend on what counts as a caller. VU2CPL divides the off-consensus spots that are not garbles into two categories, callers, which other skimmers also heard calling on that frequency, and unconfirmed calls, which only that skimmer reported there, and counts only the former as callers, whereas I count both. I reconstructed his classification from the public archive alone, and it reproduces his garble shares almost exactly and his caller and unconfirmed shares to within several points. On his definition the UberSDR Skimmer group stays at 30.7 to 34.5 percent of judged spots, against 2.0 to 2.9 percent for controls, so the finding does not depend on which definition is used.

    8.3 · Not Separable by Signal Strength

    Signal-to-noise ratio provides no usable discrimination here: spots that agree with the consensus have a median of 24 dB over 110,102 observations, and off-consensus spots a median of 25 dB over 24,079. Discarding spots below an SNR threshold slightly increases the caller share at thresholds up to 30 dB and reduces it only at 40 dB, where it also eliminates 88 percent of consensus-agreeing spots.

    8.4 · Not Visible in the Archive's Other Fields

    The RBN daily archive has maintained a stable structure of 13 columns since 2016 and contains no validity or quality indicator. Of the three fields related to quality, SNR is discussed above, and the other two are uninformative for RTTY. The speed field is a constant 45 baud, and mode reads CQ on every one of the 116,356 RTTY spots sent in the contest periods on October 10, because the Aggregator labels RTTY in that manner regardless of the content transmitted. The archive is admirably confident: by its account, every RTTY station on the air that day was calling CQ.

    9 · Every Skimmer's Frequency, Checked

    The calibration check in § 8 operates at the RBN's 100 Hz resolution and therefore detects only large errors. To detect smaller ones, and to examine every skimmer rather than only the eight, I compare each skimmer's reading of a station with the median of the other skimmers' readings of the same station, in the same band, mode, and five-minute slot, wherever at least three skimmers heard it. For each band, the offset is the mean of those differences, excluding any over 1 kHz, since the mean of many readings rounded to 100 Hz resolves more finely than the rounding itself.

    A receiver with an inaccurate sampling clock shifts every frequency by the same proportion, so I fit a single figure in parts per million across the skimmer's bands. If the offsets do not qualify as ok (defined below), I classify the skimmer's error as a clock error when the count-weighted root-mean-square residual across bands is no greater than 40 Hz. I measure a skimmer only when it has at least 30 such readings on each of at least two bands.

    The rounding nevertheless imposes a limit on each reading: 100 Hz corresponds to 3.6 ppm at 28 MHz but to 14 ppm at 7 MHz, so small per-spot errors are easier to distinguish on higher bands. Averaging many rounded readings can still resolve a smaller mean offset. The computation takes less than half a minute per contest weekend. Appendix C repeats the measurement at 10 Hz resolution for the RTTY skimmers that report it.

    10 · The Common Factor Was One Skimmer Program

    PSK Reporter lists the software names supplied by stations that reported to it during the preceding seven days. That window encompassed the Makrothen weekend when I consulted it. Comparing the list with the measured rates identifies a common program among the eight, although a listed name can identify reporting software rather than the skimmer behind it.

    PSK Reporter listed nine stations as running UberSDR Skimmer when I first consulted it on October 14. Eight of them produced sufficient RTTY on the Makrothen weekend to be judged, and those eight are precisely the eight skimmers identified in § 3. The ninth, M9PSY, is Nathan's third skimmer node and posted no RTTY. The correspondence holds in both directions:

    • No false negatives. Every ranked skimmer running UberSDR Skimmer is in the 41.8–50.2 percent group; the group's median is 44.6 percent.
    • No false positives. Every ranked skimmer above 30 percent runs UberSDR Skimmer. The highest rate among skimmers listed under other reporting software is 21.6 percent, and the controls' listed reporting programs are CW reporter, CWSL_DIGI, and VE3NEA's RTTY Skimmer Server v.1.3.0.432. The two ranked stations running Meridian v0.1.25, at 19.2 and 17.9 percent, also sit above the RTTY median, for unrelated reasons (Appendix A.1).

    A tenth, K1HTV-4, joined the UberSDR Skimmer roster on October 15, after the measurements reported above. § 12 covers its first full day of CW, and § 15 shows it carried the RTTY defect at JARL until the 18th.

    UberSDR is an open-source, web-based HF software-defined radio platform built on ka9q-radio (ubersdr.org). Its documented skimming capability is directed at FT4, FT8, and WSPR, together with an interface to VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server, and a dedicated RTTY skimmer is not among its listed features. UberSDR Skimmer is a separate add-on, ubersdr_skimmer, which its own documentation calls "a wideband CW skimmer addon for UberSDR receivers" and which "reads more than CW": it also decodes RTTY, FT8, FT4, and other modes, and sends RTTY to the RBN as RTTY. "UberSDR Skimmer" is its default name, and the one PSK Reporter lists. It is primarily a CW skimmer and secondarily an RTTY skimmer, which explains why a web search describes it as a CW skimmer, and its RTTY decoding first appears in its public documentation on October 6 and 7, days before Makrothen. The anomalous behavior went with that program: the skimmers of nine operators at different locations, counting those found with the defect at JARL, showed it while running the program, and during that contest four of those skimmers became clean, each in a single step at a different hour, which is consistent with a software update rather than with a change at the receiving station (§ 15). Because I have no instance of one operator running RTTY on UberSDR Skimmer and on another program simultaneously, I cannot exclude some contribution from the way operators configured it.

    What the software history shows, and what it does not. The live list at PSK Reporter covers only the most recent seven days, but the Internet Archive preserves earlier copies, and the one captured on September 30 covers the week of CQ WW RTTY, when K1RA and K1RA-4 were clean at 5.4 percent. It lists both under the UberSDR 0.1.66 SDR program and under CW reporter 1.1, and has no UberSDR Skimmer entry at all; K1RA is also listed under CWSL_DIGI 0.88. None of these programs is an RTTY skimmer, so which program skimmed their RTTY at CQ WW is not recorded. The copy of October 12, which covers Makrothen, lists both under UberSDR Skimmer, and CW reporter is gone. Between the two contests, therefore, they appear to have changed programs rather than merely a setting. Two limitations remain: PSK Reporter records only what reported to it, which need not be what supplied the RBN, and the version string for UberSDR Skimmer is not published, so I cannot confirm that the eight shared a single build.

    UberSDR Skimmer performs its own RTTY decoding and interpretation rather than using VE3NEA's RTTY Skimmer Server, although it reproduces that program's output formats. Its source code is private, but its documentation has been mirrored publicly since October 4 (§ 14), and the version of October 9, the day before Makrothen, describes a mechanism that would generate precisely this defect. RTTY calls were "spotted as CW's are," at the frequency of the signal they were read from, and by default the skimmer also spotted "calls heard outside CQ or DE (callers, the other side of a QSO), which go out with the type blank."

    A runner repeats each caller's callsign at the beginning of the exchange, as in "K1MK 599 …," so a skimmer interpreting the runner's signal under those rules finds K1MK in it and spots that callsign on the runner's frequency. This mechanism is consistent with every measurement above: correct callsigns, almost no garbles, no rise with consensus support, and nearly every runner affected. The documentation applied the same rules to CW, but the CW excess described in § 11 survived the RTTY fix (§ 15), suggesting that the correction did not address its cause. That does not establish whether the two modes' causes are unrelated.

    This is the behavior the documentation describes, not necessarily what the code implements. The RBN archive cannot confirm it, because it labels almost every RTTY spot as CQ, from these skimmers and the reference skimmers alike, so a spot with a blank type cannot be distinguished from a runner's; the developer's own description of the defect (§ 14) is nevertheless consistent with it.

    11 · A Smaller, Persistent Excess in CW

    These skimmers spot CW as well as RTTY, between 3246 and 14,298 CW spots each over the Makrothen weekend, so the same test can show whether they also behaved differently in CW, and they did: every one of the eight was elevated (Table 6), the excess appearing between the CQ WW and Makrothen weekends as it did in RTTY, while the controls remained unchanged.

    The CW excess is smaller, and when the RTTY defect was corrected the week of October 12 the CW excess stayed where it was (§ 15). The unchanged CW excess suggests that the RTTY correction did not address its cause. Separate causes are plausible, but the source code would be needed to establish that distinction. This section and Appendix B record what the CW spots show; none of the RTTY findings depend on it. Appendix E sets it against 15 years of the network's CW record, in which it does not show at all.

    Every CW figure here excludes the international beacon frequencies. The NCDXF/IARU beacon project places 18 beacons on a single frequency per band (14100.0, 18110.0, 21150.0, 24930.0, and 28200.0 kHz), each transmitting for 10 seconds in rotation. Because a five-minute consensus slot can designate only one holder, my method classified every other beacon heard on that frequency in that slot as off-consensus, although each spot was entirely correct.

    The artifact could not simply be subtracted, because it accounted for 65.3 percent of K3FEF's off-consensus CW spots, 56.3 percent of MM0ZBH's, and 55.6 percent of K1RA-4's, but 0.0 percent for four of the seven controls, which do not monitor those frequencies. Left in, the beacon spots would inflate the CW excesses by a factor of roughly two to three. I discovered the artifact by examining a newly added skimmer's first 23 spots individually, which is a persuasive argument for inspecting individual spots and not only aggregate rates.

    The RTTY analysis is unaffected, because the beacons transmit CW and I discard every non-RTTY spot before any grouping. Repeating §§ 3–10 with these frequencies excluded leaves the group median at 44.6 percent, since 0.86 percent of all off-consensus RTTY spots fall on a beacon frequency, and at most 1.4 percent for any individual skimmer.

    11.1 · What CW Does Not Show

    Two findings that hold in RTTY do not hold in CW, and both are significant.

    • The software no longer singles out the worst skimmers. In RTTY the eight occupied the entire top of the RTTY ranking and no other skimmer exceeded 21.6 percent, but in CW over the Makrothen weekend they do not lead: OH2BBT at 52.1 percent, OK2RZ at 51.2, K5KHK at 34.0, and OK4YL at 30.5 are all worse, and none of them runs UberSDR Skimmer. Three of those four have receiver clock errors of the kind found at VU2OY-9 (§ 8): OH2BBT at −12.9 ppm, OK2RZ at +14.9, and OK4YL at +14.7. With each error removed, their rates decline to 4.6, 3.5, and 4.1 percent, close to the CW median. K5KHK has no clock error and stays essentially unchanged, at 34.1 percent; it was also 41.1 percent in CW over the CQ WW RTTY weekend. Even after correction, a small number of skimmers running other software, K5KHK among them, remain above the UberSDR group, which is elevated within the general population of skimmers rather than at its upper extreme (Figure 6); removing every skimmer's clock error leaves the group's CW median at 7.1 percent and the controls' at 3.8, so the CW excess is not attributable to frequency error.
    • The test of whether the calls are real stations stops discriminating. In RTTY, 90 to 97 percent of the distinct callsigns each of the eight reported off-consensus were spotted by four or more skimmers, the reporting skimmer itself included, during the Makrothen periods, against 80 to 94 percent for the seven reference skimmers of Table 1, already a weak separation; Table 4, which counts spots rather than callsigns and also accepts a call that held a frequency, separates them more (97 to 98 percent against 49 to 81). In CW both lie between 77 and 89 percent. The test therefore no longer distinguishes the groups. It establishes that most discrepant callsigns belong to real stations, but does not by itself identify the mechanism; Table 7 splits the CW off-consensus spots by mechanism instead.

    That CW was affected too might have helped locate the defect: the defect may not have been just a broken RTTY decoder; it could have been something common to both modes, such as the interpretation that decides which callsign holds the frequency. The disparity is much larger in RTTY, but these weekends alone are consistent with either a shared cause or separate causes. § 15 adds evidence for operational separation: a fix that cleared RTTY left CW unchanged. Separate causes remain plausible, not established.

    11.2 · How Much Each Disturbed the Spot Stream

    How much a skimmer contributes to the stream's measured disagreement depends on two things: its off-consensus rate and how much of the stream it supplies. The RTTY excess was about 11 times larger, and the eight supplied about six times as much of the spot stream. Measured as excess off-consensus spots relative to each mode's judged traffic, the RTTY disturbance was about 65 times the CW one over the Makrothen weekend:

    • Size of the excess. In RTTY the eight ran 39.7 points above the controls (44.6 against 5.0 percent, 9.0 times; the excess is calculated from unrounded rates); in CW, 3.5 points (7.3 against 3.8 percent, 1.9 times).
    • Share of the stream. RTTY is thinly skimmed: the eight were 8 of 43 skimmers and sent 35.6 percent of all RTTY spots. In CW the same eight were 8 of 245 and sent 5.9 percent.
    • Corroboration around each spot. A decided RTTY cell had a median of 7 distinct skimmers, a CW cell 10, so an off-consensus CW spot typically has more consensus support against which to compare it.
    • Net effect. Beyond what the controls' rate would give, the eight sent about 17,800 excess off-consensus spots in RTTY, 13 percent of every judged RTTY spot, against about 1900 in CW, 0.2 percent of every judged CW spot. They supplied 83 percent of all off-consensus RTTY spots but 8 percent in CW, and left an off-consensus spot in 31 percent of decided RTTY cells against 4 percent in CW.

    The CW excess has persisted since the RTTY fix, and the UberSDR Skimmer roster's share of the CW stream has grown as more skimmers have taken up the program: over October 19–24, the 15 roster skimmers on the air sent 10.1 percent of all CW spots, and their excess was 0.3 percent of judged CW spots. It remains a small disturbance in a deep pool; the RTTY defect was a large one in a shallow pool. Appendices D and E measure the same contrast against a decade or more of each mode.

    That contrast is itself another risk of testing on the live network. How far a defect skews the total traffic depends not only on the defect but on how many other skimmers dilute it, and nobody knows either number until the spots are already out. Here the affected group supplied 5.9 percent of the stream among 245 CW skimmers but 35.6 percent among 43 RTTY ones; those are output shares, not estimates of the share attributable to a defect.

    11.3 · A CW Fix That Has Not Shown Up

    The UberSDR Skimmer development team has since treated CW as a problem of its own. The program's public changelog, kept in the mirror since October 27, lists under release v2026.10.27-3, published that day at 1331Z: "callers answering a CW runner were sometimes spotted as calling CQ on the runner's frequency." That is the caller-as-runner defect in CW. This fix followed the RTTY fix by 10 days and is the strongest sign yet that the CW excess reflects the same kind of mistake in a separate part of the program.

    The spots do not show the fix taking hold. Split the way Table 4 splits RTTY, into garbles, runners reported on the wrong frequency, callers, and unconfirmed calls, the UberSDR Skimmer roster's off-consensus CW spots are callers more than anything else, and the callers did not fall: across the 11 roster skimmers measured on both sides of the release, they made up 1.94 percent of judged spots from October 19 to 26 and 1.96 percent from October 28 to November 6, against 0.72 and 0.71 percent for the controls.     11.4 · No Other Identifiable Group

    Is UberSDR Skimmer the only group of skimmers with a CW excess? Joining PSK Reporter's software table to each skimmer's CW rate, the test that found it (§ 10), finds no other: over November 1 to 7, the 26 skimmers running UberSDR Skimmer ran a median of 6.57 percent off-consensus, with callers at 2.17 percent, against 3.37 and 0.66 percent for the 211 others with at least 1000 judged spots, and no other program differed from the rest by more than about half a point. The public records cannot tell most programs apart, however: most skimmers do not report to PSK Reporter at all, and the RBN's own node list showed nearly every skimmer on November 9, UberSDR Skimmer's included, as VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server, so the absence of another identifiable group is largely the absence of the means to identify one. The two settings the node list does record do not explain the callers either. Skimmers set to send CQ spots only report misattributed callers at nearly the same rate as those sending all spots, 0.63 against 0.73 percent: a misattributed caller goes out already labeled CQ, so neither that setting nor the Aggregator, which on HF forwards only CQ spots and beacons, can stop it. The other setting, the Validation Level, shows up in garbles instead.

    Skimmers in Asia do run high, a median off-consensus rate of 9.5 percent against 3.2 in Europe and 3.5 in North America, but that is an artifact of propagation combined with frequency reuse, not a defect: an Asian skimmer correctly hears an Asian station on a frequency that the far more numerous European and North American skimmers give to a station of their own. Appendix G gives the detail of all three findings.

    11.5 · Caller Spots Rise with Activity

    Misattributed callers are a fact of life on a busy band even without any software defect. In the first 10 hours of the 2026 ARRL November Sweepstakes CW, a bandmap with a 10-minute spot timeout, fed only by North American skimmers outside the UberSDR Skimmer roster, would typically have shown 15 to 21 misattributed callers for every 100 runners on 40, 20, and 15 m; on the quiet days of November 3 to 6 it would have shown almost none. A loud runner had such a caller in about one five-minute slot in eight at the peak, against about one in 14 on the quiet days. The roster's skimmers, about 8 percent of CW skimmers, added nearly as many again. Seeing more caller spots therefore mostly measures activity: only a skimmer group's rate, measured against controls on the same frequencies and times (§ 2), can show a defect (Appendix F). Nor is setting skimmers to send CQ spots only a remedy: a misattributed caller already carries the CQ label (§ 11.4).

    12.1 · Three Skimmers Were Dark

    Three skimmers reported no CW at all over October 12–15, a circumstance that required verification rather than assumption, because a count filtered by mode cannot distinguish a skimmer that was switched off from one that is operating and producing something else, a distinction I had already misjudged once. They were genuinely off the air: K1RA, KW4VA, and M9PSY show zero spots in any mode on all four days. M9PSY has been dark since the 10th.

    Consequently, an exclusion was less costly in practice than the estimate below suggests, because 3 of the 10 skimmers on the list were not transmitting and excluding them cost nothing while they remained inactive. They nevertheless warranted inclusion, because nothing indicated that they had been taken offline for correction, and a skimmer restarted for a contest would resume with whatever software it had been running.

    12.2 · The Cost Was Much Smaller Than the Spot Count Suggested

    On the Makrothen weekend these nine skimmers produced 68,555 of 192,802 RTTY spots, all of them from the eight, since M9PSY posted no RTTY, so excluding them would have discarded 35.6 percent of the stream, the figure that makes an exclusion appear expensive. That figure is misleading, because most of what would be discarded is either duplicated by other skimmers or was never useful.

    Across the weekend, 480 stations were reported only by these nine, a quarter of the 1906 stations seen. Of those 480 stations, 441—91.9 percent—were never identified as runners by the four-skimmer consensus test. Their spots therefore provided no confirmed exclusive runner coverage. The confirmed exclusive coverage loss is 39 running stations, 6.0 percent of the 655 that ran. Of the spots that survive the exclusion, 97.5 percent identify a station that was running somewhere.

    12.3 · Multiplier Risk for JARL: Two Entities

    JARL WW RTTY counts DXCC entities plus the call areas of W, VE, JA, and VK, per band, so the relevant question is not whether a station disappears but whether a multiplier does. Repeating the analysis of the Makrothen weekend without these nine skimmers yields the following:

    • No W or VE call area is lost on any band. All 15 areas visible from runners stay visible on 80, 40, 20, 15, and 10 m. The 39 exclusive runners include 21 W/VE stations spread over W1–W5, W7, W9, W0, and VE3, and every one of those areas is covered by another runner.
    • Two DXCC entities are lost: DU (Philippines) on 40 m through DU4WL, and GU (Guernsey) on 15 m through GU0UVH. Of the 74 entities visible from runners, 72 survive.

    The exchange is therefore approximately 20,000 off-consensus spots removed against two rare entity multipliers that would instead have to be found by tuning. On that balance the exclusion was justified for an assisted entry, and I recommended applying it to all 10 skimmers, including M9PSY (no recent CW to measure) and K1HTV-4 (one day of CW, too short to rank), because both run the same program as the other eight. The cost figures above concern the nine skimmers that were operating during Makrothen; K1HTV-4 appeared afterward, so its cost remains unmeasured. § 15 shows how quickly this advice went out of date.

    These figures come from Makrothen, not from JARL—the same skimmers in the same software state, six days earlier. Both contests are worked in every direction, which is what makes the substitution reasonable: JARL WW RTTY is a worldwide contest, and its multiplier structure treats JA, W, VE, and VK symmetrically, each divided into call areas, rather than favoring any one of them. The scoring differs, however, since Makrothen awards points by distance with no multipliers at all, whereas JARL's multipliers apply per band, so the two contests reward a different set of stations even when the same ones are audible; the structure of the finding should therefore carry over, but not the exact counts.

    13 · How the Defect Came to Light

    As far as the public record shows, the defect did not come to light through the developer's own skimmers, although two of the eight were his, nor through any verification of the program's output. On Monday, October 12, five days before JARL WW RTTY, I asked Dave K1TTT whether anything had changed on the RBN. The question reached Will WC2L, who had seen nothing about it "in any of the forums." On October 14, Will cross-posted the question to three lists: RBN-OPS, RTTY@groups.io, and Skimmertalk, along with a link to the publicly shared early draft of this document (RTTY #51007). Within hours, assisted operators were corroborating it from the receiving end. Don AA5AU reported seeing his callsign spotted on frequencies where he was answering CQ stations and said he "ended up shutting down" (#51011). Fred WZ4Z expected "a noticeable number of busted QSO's as a result of people trusting the spots" (#51018). Marcus PY2PLL saw it "always from the same group of skimmers" (#51024).

    The developer answered on RBN-OPS the next afternoon, October 15 (#9526), and a first fix followed within the hour (§ 14). On the morning of October 17, JARL's first day, Paul MM0ZBH, who runs one of the eight, confirmed "a fix for non CQ spots is in place" and passed on that the developer was "asking if anyone has a IQ file from last weekend" (#9539): a recording of the contest, which § 17 recommends having before a release, not asking for after one. Even then the correction had not reached every skimmer. N7OR's operator posted that morning, mid-contest: "It appears that I may be sending out RTTY spots of callers… I'm running an older version… I'll shut it off later this afternoon" (#9543). Table 9 (§ 15) shows N7OR clean only from the 2100Z block.

    The public record documents the mailing-list discussion, the developer's acknowledgment, and the subsequent fix. It does not establish when the development team first recognized the problem. From the spot consumer's side, nothing identified this as a test.

    An observer effect of sorts was at work: measuring the defect helped bring about its correction. The defect persisted through Makrothen; the first documented fix followed within a day of the analysis reaching the lists (§ 14). Observation works; it is simply cheaper when the developers do it themselves before a release than when the users have to do it after one.

    14 · What Changed in the Program

    Nathan M9PSY leads the development of UberSDR Skimmer. The program's source code is private, but its installation files and documentation are mirrored publicly (ubersdr_skimmer_public), and each mirror commit identifies the source commit from which it derives. Three changes correspond to the defect, the third of them in CW:

    • Mirrored October 15, 1651Z (source commit 66d8ca7). A new --rtty-contests file tells the skimmer "the words runners call CQ with (TEST, MKR, WPX...)" and what a contest report looks like. Before it, the program's documentation named CQ and DE spots but never specified how an RTTY CQ was recognized. The approach is not novel, since VE3NEA's RTTY Skimmer Server relies on the same kind of file (Contests.ini), and keeping it current is a persistent maintenance burden for RTTY Skimmer Server operators; a fixed word list is brittle, and contesters are inventive: I have seen Makrothen runners call CQ with MAK, MAKRO, and MAKROTHEN, and I am not sure any of them sends MKR. A word list is only as good as its compiler's guess about what contesters will type (and how frequently), and contesters have never been completely predictable.
    • Mirrored October 17, 2123Z (source commit a24b86f). A new default way of reading RTTY calls "keeps one call for each signal," and the skimmer stops sending PSK Reporter the calls it only read in another station's text, which "need not have been received." That matches the defect this document measures, described from within the program.
    • Released October 27, 1331Z (v2026.10.27-3, in the mirror's changelog). "Callers answering a CW runner were sometimes spotted as calling CQ on the runner's frequency": the same defect in CW, fixed 10 days after the RTTY one. § 11.3 finds no effect in the spots so far.

    The developer described the defect on the RBN-OPS list on October 15 at 1633Z, in this document's terms: "I should be able to solve the issue with callers being reported as runners," and "accuracy of decoded callsigns isn't the issue here - it's a CQ/runner issue" (Nathan M9PSY). The first change above appeared on the public mirror 18 minutes later.

    The skimmers' rates are consistent with this chronology. On October 16, a sparse day with fewer than 160 judged spots each, Nathan's own MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 were already clean, as were MM0ZBH and KC2DAC, while K5TR, KM3T-2, K1HTV-4, K1RA-4, and K3FEF were still at 24 to 37 percent. The second change followed after five of the eight were already clean, so it appears to be a refinement, although it coincides with N7OR's improvement at 2100Z on the 17th; I cannot determine when each operator installed which version, and only the UberSDR Skimmer development team can identify which source commit constituted the correction.

The next contest-sized check is WAE DX RTTY, November 14–15, and between contests, apart from the weekly windows, the honest position is hope, which is not a strategy: hope that this defect stays fixed and that no new one appears in the weeks between. What remains possible is verification during the contest itself: the RBN publishes each day's archive at approximately 0002Z the following day, so the first day of a two-day contest can be ranked in time for the second, as I did for JARL.

    16 · The Cluster's Skimmer Filters Cannot Catch It

    AR-Cluster's skimmer quality flags were added by its original author, AB5K (now a Silent Key), to a design by José CT1BOH, and the current AR-Cluster manual still calls them the "CT1BOH" quality flags. It documents SKIMVALID as being assigned "by looking back to see if there are two more spots of the same call, on the same frequency (± 0.3 kHz)." A caller inside a pileup satisfies that by construction—Table 3 shows six skimmers reporting one caller at one frequency within seconds. The spot is classified Valid, and correctly so: the callsign is real and it did transmit there.

    SKIMCQ cannot separate a caller from a runner either, being a flag for the literal string "CQ" in the comment field, which RTTY spots carry indiscriminately. Both flags behave exactly as specified. That is the trouble with specifications: they answer the question that was asked. Neither asks whether the spotted station is the one holding the frequency, which is the only question that distinguishes a caller from a runner.

    17 · Testing New Software on a Shared Network

    Development and testing can remain private while no one else depends on the output. Once that output reaches a shared network, the consequences are public. At Makrothen nothing stood in between: no test bed, no callsign of its own, no RBN policy for new skimmer software, and no filter downstream that could catch it (§ 16).

    What happened here is not new. Shared networks have been hurt before, by new software and by trusting every source, and the practices meant to prevent that grew out of those incidents.

    • 1980, ARPANET. A hardware fault in one Interface Message Processor (IMP) sent out corrupt routing updates, which every other IMP accepted and passed on. The network was unusable for hours (RFC 789). A network that trusts every source spreads one source's fault to everyone.
    • 1988, the Morris worm. A graduate student released an experimental, self-replicating program onto the internet. It reinfected machines much faster than he had anticipated, and the resulting congestion delayed instructions for stopping it (Brendan P. Kehoe's account, archived by MIT). Contemporary reporting put the affected population at an estimated 6000 computers, not a measured census (RISKS Digest 9.62, 1990 January 26). The incident led directly to the first computer emergency response team, CERT. An experiment run on the live network, rather than beside it, is out of its author's hands the moment a defect shows.
    • 1990, AT&T. New software loaded into all 114 long-distance switches in December 1989 carried a defect in handling messages from a recovering switch. A month later one switch's routine recovery set it off in the others (AT&T's explanation in Telephony, 1990 January 22, reproduced in RISKS Digest 9.62), and much of AT&T's long-distance calling failed for about nine hours (Bruce Sterling, The Hacker Crackdown, part 1). Identical code everywhere fails everywhere, so roll out in stages.

    From these come the best practices now standard wherever software runs on infrastructure other people depend on: test where nobody relies on the output; then canary, running a few nodes and comparing them against the rest (Google's Site Reliability Workbook); stage the rollout and watch between stages; label experimental output so it can be filtered; and have a way to stop it quickly.

    Measured against those practices, the UberSDR Skimmer rollout fell short. It was staged by each operator's timing, not by design, and the public record shows no systematic comparison of output between stages. By Makrothen, the eight affected skimmers supplied a third of all RTTY spots and 83 percent of the off-consensus ones. Their output was not labeled: RBN spots name no software, UberSDR Skimmer gives no version even in PSK Reporter's table, and a test build reports under the same callsign as a release. The RBN's node list goes further, identifying every UberSDR Skimmer node as VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server, version v.1.6.0.145 (Appendix G.2). A release of November 8 changed that: a node running it names the program and its release on the node list, and 14 nodes did so there by November 12, 12 with UberSDR Skimmer v2026.11.08 and 2 with the later v2026.11.12-2. Only after Makrothen, on October 17, did UberSDR Skimmer add an RBN_MODES setting that lets operators keep decoding RTTY without sending it to the RBN, while still sending CW.

    The RBN itself reflects the same trust model: like the ARPANET in 1980—the internet in its early, Wild West days—it passes skimmers' spots along and leaves the judging to whoever reads them. I found no published RBN policy for testing new skimmer software. The unwritten policy would seem to be caveat emptor: let the spot consumer beware. That alone makes the case for analyses like those in this document.

    None of this was hidden, and that is the troubling part. The program's own README has said "Status: testing." since its first public commit on October 4, six days before Makrothen; it nevertheless fed the live RBN through Makrothen and JARL. The README was candid about the software's status; the deployment ignored it. Makrothen was, in effect, an unannounced field test on the live network: I found no notice to spot consumers beforehand on RBN-OPS, the RTTY list, Skimmertalk, or the project's own ubersdr list. That made every assisted operator who relied on the spot stream in those contests an unconsulted test subject, and made the cost of each defect theirs to absorb. In addition to the direct impact on their contest operation, the cost of being an unwitting tester includes the time spent identifying the problem, tracing it back to its source, and determining to whom to report the defect—the exact process documented here. A contester should not have to suspend operating to do that work. Even afterward, that investigation requires substantial time and motivation.

    Expecting defects is a very sound attitude in a test environment. Testing on a shared network during a contest creates an externality: the developers learn, and everyone else pays. When the defect came to light, the developer's public answer on RBN-OPS was: "Apologies if it caused confusion on the weekend - nothing like a contest to find bugs" (Nathan M9PSY, October 15). The apology is welcome. The second half is a concise restatement of the problem: a contest is not a test bench, and the practices described above exist and should be followed so that it never has to be one. UberSDR Skimmer had the tools to follow them (below), and the developer's public acknowledgment followed a spot consumer's notes unintentionally appearing on three mailing lists; the public record does not establish when the development team first recognized the defect (§ 13).

    Testing RTTY does present a conundrum: outside contests there is very little RTTY activity to decode, so a new RTTY skimmer can only really be exercised in a contest, and to the operators entering it no contest is a minor event that can tolerate significant disruption. Thin RTTY coverage also magnifies any defect: with only a few dozen skimmers, instances of one affected program can supply a third of the stream (§ 11.2). The way out is to bring the contest to the test rather than the test to the contest. An SDR can record a whole band (or multiple RTTY segments) during a contest, and a new skimmer can then be run against that recording as often as needed and scored against the RBN archive for the same hours; the consensus this document uses is a ready-made yardstick. Live, a new skimmer can run in shadow mode, skimming the contest as it happens and comparing against the RBN consensus without sending anything to the RBN. Only once it has passed both tests should it publish, and then under a unique callsign, such as a separate SSID, because the callsign is all the identification of its source that a spot carries: RBN spots name no software. A version in the software's name is still worth having, but it shows only in PSK Reporter's software table, a separate feed to which not every skimmer reports (reporting there typically requires separate bridge software).

    VE3NEA's RTTY Skimmer Server, the program behind more RBN RTTY spots than any other, is a case in point. It does not report to PSK Reporter itself; a bridge such as CW reporter forwards its spots, and the table usually names only the bridge. In the week of Makrothen the table listed RTTY Skimmer Server for just two of the 43 skimmers that sent RTTY spots to the RBN that weekend, RN4WA and HA8TKS. Yet 14 more sent the 10 Hz frequencies that program produces (RBN-OPS #9243), and the 16 together supplied 38 percent of the weekend's RTTY spots, more than UberSDR Skimmer. At CQ WW RTTY in September, before UberSDR Skimmer was a factor, the skimmers sending 10 Hz frequencies alone supplied 44 percent of the RBN's RTTY spots; since 10 Hz output is optional, and RN4WA's instance, for one, reports to 100 Hz, the program's true share was likely a majority. Judged by PSK Reporter's table, it would look like a minor program.

    The tools mentioned above were already in UberSDR Skimmer's documentation before Makrothen: RBN_DRY_RUN, which signs on to the RBN and judges spots but uploads none, is in its documentation from October 4; --record and --input, which capture and replay I/Q samples, from October 5; and --compare, which holds each spot until the RBN has heard the station and then reports other:CALL "when RBN heard another call on our frequency," from October 8. That last is close to the consensus check this document uses. Tools for these safeguards shipped with the program; whether anyone used them to validate the RTTY output before sending it to the RBN, the archive cannot say.

    The guardrails were available; they did not keep this defect off the live network.

    Don't draw the wrong conclusions. The RBN needs new and innovative software, and it needs more nodes reporting RTTY spots: only 43 skimmers sent RTTY over the Makrothen weekend (§ 3), compared with 245 that sent CW (§ 11.2). UberSDR Skimmer is aimed squarely at that gap, a wideband multimode skimmer for an open-source SDR platform, and the operators behind the eight are, before anything else, volunteers putting more RTTY receivers on the air. The lead developer answered the day after the defect was raised and had a first fix on the public mirror within the hour (§ 14). Nothing here is meant as a personal criticism of anyone: the criticism is of a practice—sending untested output to a shared network—not of people. The hope is that addressing its risks will improve testing, so the next new skimmer helps contesters from its first weekend.

    18 · Summary and Conclusions

    • The callsigns were right; their attribution was not. Of the distinct callsigns each of the eight reported off-consensus, 90 to 97 percent were spotted by four or more skimmers during the contest periods, and every one was a well-formed callsign. The error lay in the spot's meaning, since a spot asserts that "this station is workable here," which during a pileup is true of the runner and false of everyone calling it, and these eight reported callers as runners on nearly half their judged spots.
    • It was a network-scale RTTY spotting failure, not a minor leak. Only 43 skimmers worldwide sent RTTY to the RBN that weekend; the eight represented nearly one in five of them, contributed a third of all RTTY spots, and generated 83 percent of the off-consensus ones, in the middle of a contest (§ 3).
    • It was new. Two of the eight measured 5.4 percent two weeks earlier in CQ WW RTTY, three others were operating then without sending any RTTY, and the program itself was absent from PSK Reporter's software table on September 30, first documented RTTY decoding on October 6–7, and was listed by October 12 (§ 10). In the Weekly RTTY Test none of its skimmers sent RTTY on October 2, and four already showed the defect on October 9 (§ 15). It also broke a decade-long trend: the network's caller share, 2 to 4 percent of judged spots in 2015 and 2016 and a median of 0.8 percent in 2023 to 2025, rose above 10 percent at Makrothen (Appendix D).
    • The common factor was one program. Every ranked skimmer running UberSDR Skimmer was among the eight, and no other skimmer approached them (§ 10); in CW, no other software group shows an excess, though the public records cannot tell most programs apart (§ 11.4). The eight instances were operated by five people, and their differing transition dates indicate an instance-by-instance rollout rather than a single misconfigured station.
    • The developer acknowledged it, and a fix followed. A spot consumer's notes appeared on three mailing lists; on October 15 the developer publicly characterized the defect as "callers being reported as runners," and the first change appeared on the public mirror 18 minutes later. By JARL WW RTTY a week later, five of the eight were clean from the outset, K1RA returned clean, and only one still showed the defect, and it too was clean by the Weekly RTTY Test of October 30. No skimmer showed the defect again, in the Russian WW Multimode contest of October 31–November 1 or in the Weekly RTTY Test of November 6. The CW excess was unchanged throughout, suggesting that the RTTY correction did not address its cause. Separate causes are plausible, but establishing them would require the source code (§§ 13–15). A CW fix for the same mistake followed on October 27, but the CW caller share has not fallen since, and over the whole of Sweepstakes it rose (§ 11.3). In the network's CW statistics, nearly flat for 15 years, the eight are diluted to invisibility (Appendix E).
    • What it shows is a missing boundary. Development and testing proceeded without an effective boundary between experimental output and the live RBN. Nothing at the level of the program, the network, or the cluster kept the two apart; until something does, nothing stops the next untested skimmer from reaching contest operators the same way (§§ 16–17).
    • The criticism is of a risky practice, not of people. The RBN needs more and better RTTY skimmers, and the volunteers who build and run them deserve encouragement; the fix is a test-first discipline, not less development (§ 17).

    19 · Scope and Limits

    • What the method measures. Off-consensus denotes disagreement with the other skimmers, not necessarily error, measured against a consensus of four in a 100 Hz, five-minute cell. Rates are expressed over judged spots. Whether a spot can be judged depends on overlapping coverage, reception, timing, frequency accuracy, and the availability of a four-skimmer consensus; where a section uses a different denominator, it states so (Appendix A.2). Stale spots can be identified only in decided cells, so their counts are lower bounds. Propagation combined with frequency reuse also defeats a consensus decided by the majority of skimmers: when two stations share a frequency in regions out of each other's reach, a correct spot from the minority region scores as off-consensus, which inflates the rates of skimmers in Asia and Oceania (§ 11.4) but not those of the eight or the controls, all in Europe and North America. Appendices D and E apply the same method, with clock errors removed, to every benchmark RTTY and CW contest the archive covers, which puts the 2026 figures on a scale of a decade or more.
    • What the spots cannot see. A spot combines site, antenna, receiver, and computer into a single receiving station (§ 6), identifies no software, and carries the time at which it was sent rather than the time at which the call was heard; frequencies are rounded to 100 Hz, apart from the RTTY skimmers that report to 10 Hz (Appendix C), so only clock errors of several ppm or more become apparent (§ 9).
    • What comes from outside the spots. Ownership, and the colocation of KM3T-2 with KM3T-5, derive from my own knowledge. The identification of each skimmer's program derives from PSK Reporter's software table, which covers only stations that report to it, over seven days, and provides no version for UberSDR Skimmer (§ 10). The mechanism derives from the program's public documentation and the developer's own statements (§§ 10, 13, and 14). Because the source code is private, which build introduced the defect and which commit corrected it remain unconfirmed.
    • What is not measured. The cost to operators, in invalid contacts, lost multipliers, and wasted time, is documented by their own reports (§ 13) but not measured here; § 12's multiplier count is a proxy. The cause of the CW excess, which persisted after the RTTY correction, and the cause of KM3T-5's latency at CQ WW have not been established.
    • Sample sizes. The weekends differ in size (1,427,694 RTTY spots in CQ WW, 192,802 in Makrothen's three periods, and 528,626 in JARL WW RTTY), so only per-skimmer comparisons are meaningful, never the aggregate difference between contests. Between contests there is too little RTTY to evaluate a skimmer except in the Weekly RTTY Test's Friday window (§ 15). Those weekly tests generally bracket RTTY changes to a week; during contests, sufficient traffic can narrow them to three-hour blocks. PSK Reporter's software table and the program's documentation provide additional chronological context (§ 10). The CW spots date the CW changes, whose relationship to the RTTY defect's cause remains unconfirmed (Appendix B.3).

    The RBN archive never keeps a trailing zero. A skimmer that reports at 10 Hz and reads 14085.30 kHz appears in the daily archive as 14085.3, and one that reads 14085.00 kHz appears as 14085. This is not new with 10 Hz frequencies: on March 1, before the RBN accepted them, whole-kHz spots already appeared as 3522, never 3522.0. The archive behaves as if each frequency were stored somewhere along the way as a binary floating-point number and written out in its shortest form, which keeps no record of how many digits were significant.

    The RBN's live telnet feed does the opposite, writing every frequency from every skimmer with two decimals, so that a 100 Hz skimmer's 7037.4 appears there as 7037.40; neither form records which kind of skimmer a spot came from.

    The two carry the same values: of 7589 spots I captured from the feed on October 30, 7580 appear in that day's archive with the identical frequency, and every one whose hundredths digit is 0 appears there without it; an hour captured during a contest on October 31, 55,864 spots, agrees as closely. The few feed spots the archive leaves out are almost all of two kinds: those of a mode the feed labels OTHER, sent without a signal report or speed, and those naming a callsign that cannot exist, such as one with an unallocated prefix. No spot in the archive ends in a hundredths digit of 0, so about one in ten spots from a 10 Hz skimmer (10.6 percent of RTTY and 12.6 percent of CW on October 17) is indistinguishable from a spot rounded to 100 Hz.

    A spot's own digits therefore cannot tell which kind of skimmer sent it. Anyone reproducing Appendix C or the frequency check of § 9 must classify each skimmer by the majority of its spots and keep all of that skimmer's spots, not select the spots that show a second decimal. Selecting spots by their digits discards a tenth of each 10 Hz skimmer's readings, all of them the ones that fall on a multiple of 100 Hz, and miscounts the totals: on October 17 it finds 86,288 RTTY spots at 10 Hz where the 22 skimmers that report at 10 Hz sent 96,484. It also makes a skimmer that switches to 10 Hz look only nine-tenths converted. The analyses retain all spots from each classified skimmer rather than filtering individual spots by decimal count.

    The majority rule labels a skimmer's output within the day or weekend being analyzed, not permanently by callsign, and does not imply uniform resolution. Appendix C.2.6 documents output that changes with frequency or time. The ramp comparisons retain eligible readings from the named group members, including their rounded readings, rather than selecting individual spots by their digits. The 100 Hz reference admits only sources with fewer than 2 percent of readings carrying a nonzero hundredths digit over the comparison period, excluding appreciably mixed output. Thus PI4CC counts as a 100 Hz reference at JARL, when all its output had that resolution, but not in the pooled 31-day reference.

    Appendix A · Four Other Anomalies, Other Causes

    Four further cases were conspicuous over these weekends for reasons of their own. None is among the eight, and none alters the main finding, but each belongs in the record. The Meridian skimmers show a different software defect: garbles rather than callers. KM3T-5 fell behind in a way the spots alone cannot explain. OK2PMU shows more ordinary decoder and clock errors. HA8TKS appears to echo, on a smaller scale, the UberSDR Skimmer rollout's use of unreleased software.

    A.1 · Meridian: Garbles and a Clock Error

    PSK Reporter's software table (§ 10) listed four stations running Meridian v0.1.25—LB9KJ-1, VU24DX-1, VU2CPL-88, and VU2OY-9—all above the RTTY median over the CQ WW weekend. The October 14 draft shared with the mailing lists treated them as a milder instance of the same effect; they are not, and I owe the correction to Manoj VU2CPL, coauthor of Meridian, who reproduced the method independently and examined the off-consensus spots against his stations' own records. They sat above the median for two distinct reasons, neither of which corresponded to my original description:

    • VU2OY-9's 86.3 percent is the frequency offset described in § 8, not misattribution at all.
    • The rest is mostly garbled decodes of the runner's own callsign—40 to 58 percent of their off-consensus spots, against 0 to 1 percent for the UberSDR Skimmer group.

    The two programs therefore show different defects, not one shared conceptual error. With respect to the caller mechanism itself, the Meridian stations occupy an intermediate position between the controls and the UberSDR Skimmer group: 3.0 and 4.2 percent of judged spots in CQ WW, 7.2 and 10.2 percent over the Makrothen weekend, against 2.0 to 4.0 percent for controls and 40.7 to 48.7 percent for the tabulated UberSDR Skimmer instances. VU2CPL's own figures for his skimmers are lower still, near the reference level, largely because he counts only confirmed callers (§ 8.2): on that definition the Meridian caller rate falls to 2.1 to 5.6 percent of judged spots, against 2.0 to 2.9 percent for controls. His stations' own logs supplied what the archive cannot show, the message context of each call, which demonstrated that the garbles follow the runner's own CQ. Both measures place VU2CPL's skimmers far below the eight, which is the comparison of consequence. Manoj reported that the next Meridian release would close the garble leak.

    A.2 · KM3T-5: A Skimmer That Fell Behind

    Manoj VU2CPL suggested examining two further stations, neither of which I had considered before he identified them. On my measure, which counts any off-consensus spot that is not a garble, KM3T-5 registers 12.8 percent in CQ WW, above every Meridian station and the highest that weekend among skimmers with at least 1000 judged spots, but only 2.0 percent over Makrothen. (The second station he suggested, OK2PMU, is the subject of A.3.)

    Over the CQ WW weekend KM3T-5 went off-consensus on 14.8 percent of its 29,311 judged spots: 12.8 points that are not garbles and 2.0 that are. Its garble rate is among the lowest of any RTTY skimmer, but on VU2CPL's stricter definition only 2.5 points of the 12.8 are confirmed callers, which corresponds to the reference level, and the remainder are not callers at all. Compared with three regional skimmers within 400 km, WC2L, WZ7I, and N2YCH, it also identified the runner in only 87.6 percent of the cells into which it spotted (its runner-naming share), against 98.4 percent over Makrothen, while theirs changed by less than two points: a deficit of 9 points relative to those peers, points specific to this skimmer and this weekend and present on all three of its bands. (WC2L posted no RTTY on September 26, so that day's hourly comparisons rely on the other two.)

    A.2.1 · First Explanation Tried: The Contest and the Weather

    The size of the contest was not responsible: a year earlier, during a busier CQ WW (2.25 million RTTY spots, against 1.43 million), KM3T-5 matched its peers to within a point, and it did so to within 1.2 points on every other 2025–26 RTTY weekend on which a comparison was possible.

    A nor'easter passed off the southern New England coast that weekend, and I suspected it of triggering the fault through precipitation static, lightning, or wind loosening hardware sufficiently to cause arcing. I tested each possibility against the hours in which the deficit appeared, which are identified in the next subsection:

    • Rain, from the four nearest working gauges (20 to 44 km): rain fell in 46 of the weekend's 47 judged hours, and the wettest hour, 0.30 inches, coincided with normal reception, whereas other hours of 0.22 to 0.25 inches did not.
    • Wind, from the four nearest stations: of 24 hours with gusts of 30 to 45 knots, 10 were affected and 14 were normal, including all 8 with the strongest gusts, so gust strength and the deficit are uncorrelated.
    • Lightning nearby, from the surface network and the GOES-East satellite's lightning mapper: the first day's worst hours coincided with the storm's closest approach, approximately 100 km, but comparable lightning earlier that day left reception unaffected, and the second day's 10 affected hours had almost no lightning within 600 km.
    • Lightning by skywave, from storms out past 6000 km, compared hour by hour across the two geomagnetically quiet days: no range of distance tracked the deficit, and hours with comparable distant lightning produced opposite results.
    • The same site: KM3T-2, a second skimmer at the same QTH (see below), was unaffected in the same weather.

    Each test weakened the weather hypothesis without ruling it out. Noise received by KM3T-5's own antenna but not by its neighbor's could still have overwhelmed its decoder, so the investigation did not yield a definitive answer.

    A.2.2 · What KM3T-5's Spots Actually Were

    Of KM3T-5's 3766 off-consensus spots in CQ WW that are not garbles, only 20 percent identify a caller that other skimmers also heard calling there. Half name a station that was itself running somewhere else at the time—against 0 to 3 percent for its peers, and 2 percent for KM3T-5 itself over Makrothen—and 44 percent of those appear on a different band from the one on which the station was running. A further 14 percent name a station that no other skimmer reported at all, against approximately 1 percent for the peers. The frequencies do not account for this pattern, since there is no offset and no harmonic, mixing, or image relationship between the frequency at which KM3T-5 placed a station and the frequency the station actually occupied.

    Timing does account for it. In more than half of the 3766 spots, 2025 of them, KM3T-5 put a station on the exact frequency that station had itself run on earlier (a median of 55 minutes earlier, and more than an hour in 803 cases), after another station had occupied it; I call these stale spots. Its three peers together produced approximately 90 such spots, typically five minutes old, and the 663 stations involved are predominantly widely heard DX stations rather than local ones that might have remained audible only in New Hampshire. The skimmer was therefore reporting stations at frequencies they had already vacated.

    The same skimmer's CW output exhibits the same fault in a milder form: in the hours concerned, its CW spots arrived 162 to 412 seconds behind the first skimmer to hear the same runner, against 7 to 228 seconds for the other skimmers in the comparison, and it produced 171 stale CW spots, against 0 to 16 for its peers. A skimmer that has fallen behind reports a station late, and a sufficiently late report places it on a frequency it has already vacated.

    During CQ WW RTTY, which ran continuously from 0000Z on September 26 to 2400Z on the 27th, the fault occupied much the same hours on both days. In KM3T-5's RTTY output, it began at 1400Z on the 26th (10 a.m. local, Eastern Daylight Time) and was fully on from 1500Z until 2200Z (6 p.m. local); on the 27th it began an hour earlier, at 1300Z, was fully on from 1400Z, and was still on when the contest ended at 2400Z (8 p.m. local). Each onset took about an hour; the first episode also ended within about an hour, and the second continued through the end of the contest. Outside the observed episodes KM3T-5 performed as well as its peers, while its CW latency was elevated over approximately the same intervals. Ninety-six percent of the identified stale spots fell in those hours; during those hours, stale spots were about 7 percent of all output from KM3T-5, compared with 0.1 percent during its other hours; the share reached 13 percent in one half-hour (Figure 8). That is a lower bound: a spot can be recognized as stale only where it lands in a cell the consensus decided, and in those hours only 29 percent of its spots did, against 82 percent in its other hours. Counted over the spots that could be checked, the measure the runner-naming deficit uses, stale spots were 28.0 percent, enough by themselves to account for most of the deficit.

    Across the weekend, its stale spots, 2025 in all, were 3.8 percent of the 53,636 RTTY spots KM3T-5 sent—well below the eight's off-consensus share at Makrothen, 29 percent (20,009 of 68,555), but consequential while the backlog lasted. A skimmer can read every call correctly and still report it an hour late, which would not be a problem if the spot carried the time the call was heard. It does not: KM3T-5's late spots are stamped with the time they were sent, and that is where the problem lies. The disturbance was confined to about 18 hours, but substantial during those hours.

    The fault was confined to the CQ WW weekend. Over Makrothen, two weeks later, it had disappeared, or at least did not manifest itself: stale spots were 0.2 percent of KM3T-5's RTTY output, with no hour above 2 percent; it named the runner 98.4 percent of the time; and its CW spots were as prompt as any skimmer's. It stayed absent at JARL WW RTTY a week after that: 5 stale spots in 8985 RTTY spots, none more than a few minutes late. Its runner-naming shows nothing like it on any other RTTY weekend in its 2025–26 record either.

    A second skimmer at the same site helps narrow where the fault originated. KM3T-2 runs at Dave KM3T's QTH alongside KM3T-5, on its own receiver and antenna, and during CQ WW decoded CW only. It was clean in every hour of the weekend: its CW runner-naming share stayed within about five points of the regional peers' throughout, with 5 stale spots in 6108. (KM3T-2 has since moved to UberSDR Skimmer: PSK Reporter listed it by October 17, and at JARL its RTTY was elevated until midday on the 17th.) The two installations shared the site and broad weather conditions, but their separate antennas and receivers could respond differently. Their contrasting behavior points toward a fault specific to KM3T-5's installation, which the spots cannot divide into receiver, antenna, and computer, although simultaneous latency in two modes is characteristic of a processing backlog.

    The RBN archive does not record what software a skimmer ran, and KM3T-5 was not reporting to PSK Reporter during CQ WW, so no record I have found establishes what it ran then. I have not determined the cause of the fault. With peak latencies exceeding an hour, it is difficult to regard it as anything other than a processing problem.

    A.3 · OK2PMU: A Weaker Decoder and a Drifting Clock

    OK2PMU is a lesser and more ordinary case. It was absent on every day checked before September 25, including CQ WW RTTY 2025, CQ WPX RTTY 2026, and SARTG in August, came on the air at 1912Z on September 25, the eve of CQ WW RTTY, and sent 69,346 RTTY spots over the contest weekend, more than KM3T-5. It then spotted CW daily, with a little RTTY, until October 10, and again from October 14 to 16, and has sent nothing since. It sat out both later contests in RTTY: on October 10, the first day of Makrothen, it sent 7765 CW spots and no RTTY, stopping at 2100Z, and nothing on the 11th; at JARL it was off the air altogether. CQ WW RTTY was the only contest in which it decoded RTTY. It never appears in PSK Reporter's software table, so its software is unknown.

    Its 10.2 percent off-consensus rate at CQ WW had two components. The 4.6 points of garbles, its decoder's own misreadings of the runner's call, are about twice the 1.9 to 2.4 percent of the two regional reference skimmers with samples as large, S50ARX and OE9GHV: a somewhat weaker RTTY decoder.

    Of the 5.5 points that are not garbles, about a third were near misses: a call the other skimmers heard, reported one 100 Hz cell away, and consistently high, by 100 Hz on 40 and 20 m and 200 Hz on 15 m. An offset that increases with frequency indicates a receiver clock error, and this one increased over time: about +1 to +3 ppm averaged over CQ WW, then +8.7 ppm in CW by October 10 (Table 5), when that error alone raised its CW rate from 10.4 percent, the rate with the error removed, to 16.1.

    It was not delayed: its spots arrived as promptly as its peers', and stale spots constituted 0.5 percent of its judged spots, distributed evenly throughout the weekend.

    A.4 · HA8TKS and Ditty

    The ninth-worst skimmer over the Makrothen weekend (§ 3) sits in the same gap again at JARL: HA8TKS, off-consensus on 21.6 percent of 1521 judged RTTY spots at Makrothen and 18.2 percent of 4869 at JARL. Its off-consensus spots are not garbles of the Meridian kind: only 2 to 3 percent are garbles of the runner's callsign, and the remainder are callers, stations running elsewhere, and calls that no other skimmer reported. Those last make up 31 percent of its off-consensus spots at Makrothen and 58 percent at JARL, and 89 to 96 percent of them are real callsigns. That is the characteristic signature of the eight at approximately half the intensity.

    PSK Reporter's software table, in every copy I have from October 12 onward (those of October 12, 14, 15, 17, 19, and 21), lists HA8TKS as the only user anywhere of Ditty_RTTY_Skimmer 1.0; the copies of September 16 and 30 list no RTTY program for it at all. Ditty is its operator's own multimode skimmer (ditty.ha8tks.hu), whose RTTY part does "callsign extraction from RTTY transmissions." By its author's own account it was unreleased: "software release not yet published," with documentation that "is not yet a validated end-user installation manual." Its site does not explain how its RTTY skimmer distinguishes a CQ from a caller.

    One qualification is important. The copies of October 12, 14, and 15, each covering the preceding seven days and together October 6–15, the Makrothen weekend included, also list HA8TKS under two other RTTY programs, CWSL_RTTY 1.0 and RTTY Skimmer Server, and neither the RBN archive nor the Ditty site indicates which of them supplied the RBN in that period. The signature argues against RTTY Skimmer Server, whose users run clean, but the spots alone cannot resolve the question for Makrothen. The later copies narrow it for JARL: those of October 17, 19, and 21, the second of them covering October 13–19 and so the whole JARL weekend, list Ditty as its only RTTY program. PSK Reporter records what software reported to it, not which program fed the RBN, but on that evidence Ditty is the likelier source of its JARL spots. If Ditty supplied them, this is the same history as that of UberSDR Skimmer on a smaller scale: software its own author has not yet released or validated, skimming RTTY for the live RBN through two contests (§ 17).

    Appendix B · The CW Excess in Detail

    This appendix holds the CW checks moved out of § 11. The CW excess persisted after the RTTY correction; whether the two have separate causes remains unconfirmed. None of the RTTY findings depend on what follows. Appendix E sets the CW excess against the network's long-term CW record.

    B.1 · Not an Artifact of the Frequency Bin

    CW callers frequently transmit a few hundred hertz off frequency in order to be heard, which could place a caller outside the runner's own 100 Hz bin and conceal it from this test, the obvious explanation for why a genuine CW problem might appear mild. Repeating the analysis with tolerances of ±200 and ±500 Hz excludes that possibility: widening the bin raises every skimmer's rate, because it also combines genuinely separate stations, but it reduces the disparity rather than enlarging it, the group-to-control ratio falling from 1.9 to 1.7 to 1.4. There are no concealed offset callers to discover, and the narrow bin is the more discriminating measurement; on the CQ WW weekend the ratio is 1.1, 1.0, and 0.9 at the three widths, with no excess at any of them.

    B.2 · Not a Matter of Contest Load

    The weekend after Makrothen tested whether the CW excess grows with the traffic. WAG (Worked All Germany), on 80, 40, 20, 15, and 10 m, and the Stew Perry Topband Challenge, on 160 m only, both ran from 1500Z on October 17 to 1500Z on the 18th, in the middle of JARL WW RTTY. I compared that window with the same 24 hours, 1500Z to 1500Z, on eight quieter days from October 13 to 21, judging each contest on its own bands and setting the skimmers on the UberSDR Skimmer roster against the seven reference skimmers:

    • WAG's bands: no change. With about six times a quiet day's judged traffic (163,169 of the roster's spots, against 20,759 to 33,193), the roster's median was 6.3 percent against 3.1 for the controls, an excess of 3.2 points (2.0 times), inside the quiet days' range of 2.4 to 4.0 points (1.5 to 2.1 times). As in RTTY (§ 5), a busier band does not make the defect worse. Sweepstakes, whose exchanges begin with the caller's callsign, did raise the caller share (§ 11.3).
    • The Stew Perry, on 160 m: almost none. The roster's median was 3.0 percent against 2.7 (1.1 times), on 11,948 of its judged spots, far below its excess on the other bands. On quiet days 160 m carries too few decided cells to compare, and the spots do not show why the band behaves differently.

    pp = percentage points: each skimmer's daily CW off-consensus rate minus that day's control median, averaged over the days before and after its transition, weighted by judged spots.

    The standardized separation statistic divides the change in count-weighted segment means by the square root of the sum of each segment's count-weighted variance divided by its number of observed days. The breakpoint is selected from the same data to minimize the within-segment weighted sum of squares. This is a descriptive measure of separation, not a conventional Welch significance test; no p-value is inferred. "Nonoverlapping" means the highest daily excess before the transition is below the lowest afterward.

    The two sharpest transitions constitute the strongest evidence for a released build rather than individual experimentation: K1RA-4 and MM0ZBH both step between October 5 and 6, from +0.1 and +0.7 points to +4.2 and +2.8, over 11 clean days before and 6 elevated days after, with no overlap between the two segments, and they belong to different operators. Excluding the beacon frequencies reduced the steps and left three transition dates unchanged; MM9PSY's moved one day earlier.

    The four datable transitions occur in two pairs, two on October 2–3 and two on October 5–6, a distribution consistent with the adoption of a new version as operators update at their own pace, rather than of a single setting changing everywhere at once. The four undatable skimmers conform to the same pattern: two of them (M9PSY-2, KW4VA) were entirely absent for the fortnight before they appeared and were already affected when they began reporting, which is the expected behavior of a skimmer started on an installation that had already been updated.

    The transition dates indicate an instance-by-instance rollout. § 3 groups the eight instances under five operators. The dating demonstrates that instances under one operator did not change simultaneously: Andy K1RA's K1RA-4 steps on October 5–6 while his KW4VA first appears on October 10, and Nathan M9PSY's MM9PSY steps on October 2–3 while his M9PSY shows no step and his M9PSY-2 arrives on the 9th. The rollout therefore proceeded by instance, not by operator; these dates do not establish statistical independence among the observations.

    Binary search was the wrong tool, and I have left the record here deliberately. Bisecting the group median read six archive days, September 27 and 30 and October 1, 2, 3, and 10, four of them fetched for the purpose, and converged on October 2–3. That boundary is an artifact, because the median includes whichever skimmers exceed the judged-spot threshold on a given day, and K3FEF and M9PSY first appear on October 3 already elevated, which moves the median without any skimmer changing—two of the four skimmers measured on both days actually went down that day. Bisection also presupposes a single monotonic step, and there is none; the per-skimmer analysis of all 17 days resolved the question instead and, with eight of them already available, required nine additional archive days, approximately 38 MB.

    Appendix C · RTTY Frequencies at 10 Hz Resolution

    On October 17, the first day of JARL, about a third of all RTTY spots came from skimmers reporting at 10 Hz: 96,484 of 271,834, from 22 skimmers. The RBN began accepting that resolution in March 2026 (§ 2). CW is different: only 5263 of the 1,260,334 CW spots that day came from 10 Hz skimmers, all of them the four Meridian stations. The archive drops a trailing zero, writing a reading of 14085.30 kHz as 14085.3 and one of 14085.00 kHz as 14085, so about one in ten of a 10 Hz skimmer's spots (10.6 percent in RTTY and 12.6 percent in CW on October 17) looks exactly like a 100 Hz spot. I therefore identify each 10 Hz skimmer from the majority of its spots and keep all of them (§ 20). A frequency resolved to 10 Hz rather than 100 Hz allows the frequency check of § 9 to be repeated at 10 times the resolution, for the RTTY skimmers that report it: 28 over the CQ WW weekend, 16 over the Makrothen weekend, and 22 at JARL. Nothing here changes the main findings, but the finer view shows two things the 100 Hz check could not.

    C.1 · Receiver Clocks, Resolved to About 1 ppm

    I compare each skimmer's reading of a station only with the readings of the other 10 Hz skimmers that heard the same station, band, and five-minute slot, so no 100 Hz rounding enters the reference, and fit a clock error across bands as in § 9. Errors of 1 to 2 ppm become visible, and those that SM7IUN's independent CW figures confirm are real receiver errors:

    • VU2OY-9 fits +12.4 to +13.3 ppm on all three weekends with a scatter of only 1 to 4 Hz; SM7IUN has it at +11.9 and +13.6 on the JARL days and near zero from October 22, so the calibration fix of § 8 reached it after JARL.
    • LB9KJ-1 drifts: +0.5 ppm over CQ WW, +2.4 over Makrothen, and +5.6 at JARL. The 100 Hz check saw it only at JARL (Table 5). SM7IUN shows it reaching +6.9 on October 22–23 and corrected to +0.2 on October 25.
    • HS0ZGZ runs about +1 to +2 ppm, below Table 5's 3 ppm threshold.

    A fit to RTTY at this resolution is not, however, a clock measurement by itself. Two skimmers whose RTTY fits suggested about +2.7 and −2.4 ppm, WZ7I and DL9GTB, are within about 1 ppm in SM7IUN's CW figures; their RTTY offsets are the band-by-band pattern of C.2, not a clock. A 10 Hz fit needs an independent CW figure beside it before it is called a clock error.

    C.2 · A Ramp in RTTY Frequency across Each Band

    C.2.1 · The Two Groups

    For most 10 Hz skimmers, a single clock-error model does not fit the observed frequency offsets well, and the reason is systematic. They fall into two groups that agree within themselves to about 10 Hz but disagree with each other, and the disagreement is not a constant: it is a straight-line ramp across each band, with nearly the same slope on every band. On 40 m, the negative-slope group reads about 90 Hz above the positive-slope group at 7030 kHz. They agree near 7085 kHz; by 7100 kHz, the negative-slope group reads about 10 Hz below the positive-slope group. This is a scale difference of about 0.15 percent of a signal's distance from the agreement point. On 20 m, the negative-slope group's lead likewise gives way to a lag: the difference runs from +20 Hz at 14070 kHz to −90 Hz at 14140 kHz, about 0.17 percent.

    The same difference appears on every contest band: fitted with each bin weighted by its readings, it is 0.16 to 0.17 percent on 40, 20, and 15 m, 0.13 percent on 80 m, where activity fills mainly the top of the segment, and 0.19 percent on 10 m, where few of these skimmers report (Figure 10). Averaged over each band the ramp looks like a band-dependent offset, about +60 Hz on 40 and 30 m, none on 80 m, and −5 to −20 Hz on 20, 15, and 10 m, only because most activity sits low in the 40 m segment and near the middle of the others.

    The two groups agree at a definite frequency on each band, where their fitted lines cross: 3584, 7084, 14080, 21078, and 28076 kHz, each 76 to 84 kHz above the band's lower edge. That is where a receiver sampling at 192 kHz would be centered to cover both the CW and the RTTY parts of a band, and the skimmers' own coverage shows they are set up that way. On 20 and 15 m nearly every one reports CW and RTTY together across 130 to 150 kHz, more than a 96 kHz receiver spans, so they sample at 192 kHz.

    On 10 m, those that use a single receiver report down to the band edge but nothing above about 28170 kHz, not even the beacons from 28190 kHz up that other skimmers log thousands of times; the two whose reports reach highest, AC0C-1 and VE3EID, one from each group, must be centered between about 28071 and 28096 kHz, around the 28076 kHz agreement point. These are not RTTY Skimmer Server's own default centers, 3575, 7065, 14105, 21105, and 28105 kHz, but published setup guidance has operators match the RTTY skimmer's centers to the CW skimmer's. The agreement points also coincide with the lower edges of the program's default non-contest RTTY segments, but only numerically: every one of these skimmers decodes RTTY outside those segments, on quiet days as well as in contests, so none runs with them. The JA window near 3530 kHz extends the 80 m baseline for the positive-slope group only: the negative-slope group's skimmers do not decode below about 3560 kHz.

    Measured against an independent reference, the 49 skimmers that report only to 100 Hz (whose rounding averages out over many spots), both groups ramp, in opposite directions (Figure 10):

    • Negative-slope group, about −0.04 to −0.07 percent: KM3T-5, LZ4UX, LY2BGP, AC0C-1, OE9GHV, PI4CC, PC2A, DL9GTB, OH8KA, GX0FRE, and GI4DOH.
    • Positive-slope group, about +0.07 to +0.13 percent: S50U, N6TV, NG7M, NN3RP, G4ZFE, HS0ZGZ, WZ7I, KH6LC, WC2L, YO5LD, PA5KT-4, and VE3EID.

    Three of SM7IUN's GPS-referenced reference skimmers, KM3T-5, OE9GHV, and LZ4UX, belong to the negative-slope group. The Meridian stations sit near zero. In each group the error is proportional to a signal's frequency offset from the receiver's center frequency, near which the two groups agree, with a scale factor of a few hundredths to about a tenth of a percent (a slope of 1 Hz per kHz is 0.1 percent). That is not a clock error. A clock error scales absolute frequency, so it shifts a whole 40 m segment by nearly the same amount, 7 Hz per ppm, and would affect CW identically. Nor can FreqCalibration, the program's single global correction factor, remove it, since it too multiplies absolute frequency.

    C.2.2 · Why the Shape Points to Processing

    The shape points to where in the processing the error enters. A skimmer of this kind takes the receiver's I/Q, centered on the tuned frequency, channelizes it with a fast Fourier transform (FFT), and places each decoded tone at the center frequency plus an offset computed from its bin number and the bin width, or from the sample rate it assumes for a decimated channel, refined by interpolating between adjacent bins. Of the mechanisms considered here, a scale mismatch in the offset calculation best fits this ramp. A clock error scales absolute frequency, as above. An interpolation error is confined within a bin and repeats from bin to bin, a fraction of a bin at most, so it cannot build a ramp across 70 kHz. A mismatch between the sampling rate or bin width the program assumes and the one its own arithmetic produces does: it scales every offset from the center by the same fraction, with zero error at the center, which is where the two groups agree. The I/Q itself arrives at exactly the rate the program is told (C.3), so such a mismatch would lie in RTTY Skimmer Server's own bookkeeping; the difference between the groups, about 0.16 percent or one part in 600 to 625, is the size an integer rounding of an FFT length or decimation ratio would give. RTTY Skimmer Server's internals are not published, so this is an inference from the shape. Comparing skimmers with one another cannot say which group's arithmetic is the true one, but the stations' own frequencies can, and they favor the negative-slope group (below).

    C.2.3 · Stability across Seven Months

    The pattern has held since the RBN first accepted 10 Hz frequencies. Repeating the analysis separately on each of nine RTTY contest weekends, from YB DX and the North American Sprint on March 14–15 through BARTG HF, EA, IG-RY, SP DX, VOLTA, CQ WW, and Makrothen to JARL on October 17–18, finds the same two groups every time. As few as 7 skimmers reported at 10 Hz in March and as many as 28 at CQ WW, but each one that appears on two or more weekends keeps its group's sign of slope on every weekend but one (OH8KA on April 4–5). Each group's slope against the 100 Hz reference does move, by about the same amount in the same direction for both groups, but that reference is a mixture of 15 to 35 skimmers that changes from weekend to weekend, and only four of them qualify on all nine.

    A test of constancy has to hold the membership fixed and leave the reference out. Comparing two skimmers directly, in the slots where both heard the same station, does both: the membership is fixed and no reference enters. So does comparing three fixed members of each group together. AC0C-1, the one member of the negative-slope group present on all nine weekends, differs from N6TV and WZ7I (all nine) and G4ZFE (eight) by 0.146 to 0.171 percent on every weekend, nearly always within 0.01 percentage points of 0.16 percent, or about one part in 620. The frequency where AC0C-1, KM3T-5, and OE9GHV agree with N6TV, WZ7I, and G4ZFE stays put too: 7077 to 7086 kHz on 40 m, 14075 to 14083 kHz on 20 m, and 21066 to 21079 kHz on 15 m, on every weekend with enough data. The scale difference and the frequency where it vanishes are therefore fixed properties of the two groups' processing, unchanged across seven months, consistent with a fixed setting rather than drift, wear, or an update.

    The one exception is KM3T-5. Against N6TV and WZ7I it differed by 0.17 percent on March 14–15, like AC0C-1, but by 0.26 to 0.34 percent from March 21 through April, 0.20 to 0.25 at CQ WW and Makrothen, and 0.31 at JARL. Its sign never changed, but its size did, more than once, so something at that station changed while the others held constant; as one of SM7IUN's GPS-referenced anchors, it has no clock error to explain it.

    C.2.4 · The Stations' Frequency Pattern

    The hundredths digits are not spread evenly, because the stations are not. Their signals pile up at a common point a little below a multiple of 100 Hz, presumably because many operators select round dial settings for their transmissions. That concentration in transmitted frequencies provides an independent reference against which to assess the skimmers' readings. Over CQ WW and JARL, the negative-slope group shows it plainly: the digits 8, 9, and 0 make up 47 percent of its readings, where an even spread would give 30, and the overdensity sits 85 to 90 Hz past each 100 Hz step at every distance from the agreement point, from 50 kHz below it to 50 kHz above. The positive-slope group's digits look nearly flat, 9 to 11 percent each apart from 0, but only because its ramp slides the pile around the 100 Hz cycle as a signal's distance from the agreement point changes; near that point its pile sits at 89 Hz too. For each skimmer, I estimate the scale error by finding the adjustment that most strongly restores the concentration of reported frequencies. For LZ4UX, LY2BGP, AC0C-1, OE9GHV, and GX0FRE the estimated scale error is zero within 0.005 percent, and for the negative-slope group as a whole it is zero. For every member of the positive-slope group the estimated scale error is +0.150 to +0.175 percent, and +0.160 percent for the group, the whole of the difference between the groups. The adjustment subtracts the estimated distortion from each reported frequency, reducing a positive stretch rather than adding to it. Applying that adjustment raises the pile's concentration (0 for an even spread, 1 for a single value) from between 0.06 and 0.10 to between 0.25 and 0.38. If the pile belongs to transmitting stations, and is not an artifact the two groups share (assuming that both run RTTY Skimmer Server; the Meridian stations, running a different program, show it at 94 to 98 Hz with almost no correction), the negative-slope group reads true, and the positive-slope group's readings are stretched by about 0.16 percent of their distance from a point near the agreement point. The 100 Hz reference of Figure 10, against which the negative-slope group ramps by −0.04 to −0.07 percent, is then itself stretched by about that much, as a mixture of programs can be.

    C.2.5 · An Independent Radio Check

    Radios independent of the RBN give nearly the same answer without any assumption about how stations select their transmitting frequencies. The logs of the seven RTTY contests I have operated since March record each contact's frequency in the logging program's database (the Cabrillo files sent to the sponsors keep only the whole kilohertz). All seven were operated from the same station, K1TTT's, with the same radios and software. Six are mine as a single operator, working the station remotely, so that every change of frequency goes through the logging program and moves in its 20 Hz tuning steps: SP DX, VOLTA, SARTG, Russian WW, Makrothen, and JARL. The seventh, CQ WW, is the multi-operator log of K1SFA, with a mix of remote and on-site operators, I among them (the on-site operators were free to tune the radios directly without fixed N1MM tuning steps), and K1SFA used a somewhat different N1MM configuration.

    In the 3429 contacts made searching and pouncing rather than running, the logged frequency is where the operator tuned to the other station. I matched each against the same station's spots on the same band within 10 minutes and left out every contact whose logged frequency equals some skimmer's spot of it, since the operator may have tuned those by clicking on the spot; about 1900 remain with readings from both groups.

    Against these radios the positive-slope group is stretched by (0.138 ± 0.011)% of a signal's distance from the agreement point (quoted uncertainties are one standard error of the fitted regression slope, not confidence intervals), and by 0.140 to 0.191 percent in each of the seven contests separately, including (0.140 ± 0.013)% in K1SFA's log, the most precise of them. The negative-slope group reads (0.038 ± 0.011)% short, and the 100 Hz reference 0.05 percent long.

    A calibration error in the station's radios would scale absolute frequency, moving every reading across a band by nearly the same amount, and the point of the signal the logging program records adds only a constant, here about 60 Hz; neither changes a slope. The stretch is therefore mostly the positive-slope group's, about 0.14 to 0.16 percent, with a smaller estimated compression of about 0.04 percent in the negative-slope group.

    C.2.6 · Rounding and Mixed Resolution

    Two kinds of rounding also show in the digits. The 100 Hz skimmers round to the nearest step rather than truncating: near the agreement point, where the two groups of 10 Hz skimmers agree, 85 percent of their readings fall within 50 Hz of the 10 Hz readings in the same slot, centered at +10 Hz rather than the −50 Hz truncation would give, and the +10 Hz is the pile at 90 Hz rounding up. A few 100 Hz skimmers, among them PY2KNK, 7L4IOU, ZF1A, and KM7W, sit about 40 to 75 Hz off, which truncation or a clock error could each produce; these data cannot separate the two.

    Some 10 Hz skimmers, in turn, send part of their spots rounded to 100 Hz. Because the archive drops a trailing zero (§ 20), about a tenth of a 10 Hz skimmer's spots should show no hundredths digit, and most show 8 to 13 percent, but over CQ WW and JARL PI4CC shows 44, PC2A 56, G4ZFE 26, and GI4DOH 25 percent. Their spots without a hundredths digit scatter about the other skimmers of their group roughly twice as widely as their other spots (a standard deviation of 21 to 30 Hz against 13 to 17 Hz), as rounding to 100 Hz would make them.

    The rounding follows two different patterns. At PI4CC and PC2A it follows frequency: at CQ WW each reported at 10 Hz up to about 75 to 95 kHz above the band's lower edge and only at 100 Hz from there to the top of its coverage (at PI4CC, from about 3585, 7080, 14095, and 21095 kHz up to 3600, 7100, 14100, and 21100 kHz), with a narrow overlap in which the same station was often reported twice within two minutes, once at each resolution, 1355 times at PI4CC. That is the signature of two sources reporting under one callsign, consistent with the two banks of eight receivers PI4CC's own description lists. Its output also changed from weekend to weekend: mixed at CQ WW, entirely at 10 Hz at Makrothen, and entirely at 100 Hz at JARL, where it therefore counted among the 100 Hz reference skimmers.

    At G4ZFE and GI4DOH the rounding follows time instead: about half their spots were rounded in the first hours of CQ WW, until about 1300Z and 0700Z on September 26, with no dependence on frequency and almost no such duplicates, and both have reported at 10 Hz alone since, through Makrothen and JARL.

    KM3T-5 also shows an excess of spots without a hundredths digit, 33 percent, but those spots scatter no more widely than its others, so they are not rounded. Rounded spots add scatter and at most a few hertz of constant offset, not a ramp, so they do not affect the slopes or agreement points above.

    C.3 · What the Ramp Is Not

    • Not the receivers. Three of SM7IUN's GPS-referenced anchor skimmers (KM3T-5, OE9GHV, and LZ4UX) are in the negative-slope group, and apart from the clock errors in C.1 and Table 5, the members of both groups that he measures are within about 0.6 ppm in his CW figures for October 16–25. In CW, the same stations report the NCDXF/IARU beacons at exactly 14100.0, 18110.0, 21150.0, and 24930.0 kHz, and N6TV and KH6LC at exactly 28200.0 kHz. The 21150.0 kHz beacon lies 72 kHz above the 15 m agreement point, where the positive-slope group's RTTY scale would put it about 115 Hz high, more than a full 100 Hz step; neither group's CW reading moves. The ramp is therefore confined to RTTY.
    • Not a sideband convention. RTTY is lower sideband on every band, and 30 m, above 10 MHz, behaves like 40 m: in every slot both groups shared there, G4ZFE read 50 to 70 Hz below KM3T-5 and OE9GHV, as on 40 m and unlike 20 m. (30 m RTTY is scarce on the RBN, 189 spots in 17 days, so this rests on one skimmer.)
    • Not a per-band setting. RTTY Skimmer Server has none that could do it: its manual lists one global FreqCalibration factor, and its only per-band settings, the center frequencies and segments, set coverage.
    • Not a simple sample-rate error in the firmware, I/Q-sharing layer, or driver. Pavel Demin's receiver firmware cannot produce a scale error of this kind, even though it decimates by large factors. Dividing a sample clock by a whole number gives exactly the clock rate over that number; rounding enters only when software assumes a rate the hardware does not deliver, and nothing here is rounded. The 125-14 divides 125 MHz by 250 to 1000 and then resamples by exactly 24/25 and 2/5 (125 MHz ÷ 250 × 24/25 × 2/5 is 192,000 Hz); the 122.88-16 divides by 160 to 1280 and then by 2; and every release of HermesIntf since 2016 tells the skimmer the same nominal rates the firmware delivers. Each board is tuned with its own clock through a 32-bit phase word, about 0.03 Hz a step, far too fine to matter. A wrong output rate would, moreover, affect CW from the same receiver equally, yet N6TV, on a 122.88-16, reports the 21150.0 kHz beacon exactly. A rounding of the size measured would have to sit after the I/Q arrives, in the skimmer's own channelization. CWSL, which lets several programs share one receiver, passes the I/Q through unchanged and refuses a mismatched sample rate.
    • Not a recent software release. The only public build of RTTY Skimmer Server dates from February 2018, so no recent update can explain it.

    The hardware does not sort the two groups cleanly either. G4ZFE, N6TV, and NG7M, all in the positive-slope group, run the 122.88-16 Red Pitaya, but the negative-slope group includes a Hermes-Lite (OH8KA) and a station (OE9GHV) that runs both Red Pitaya models. What remains is RTTY Skimmer Server's own processing: a scale on each tone's offset from the receiver's center frequency that differs between the two groups by about 0.16 percent, carried by the positive-slope group (C.2). The inputs it works from look the same on both sides. The I/Q arrives at exactly the rate the program is told; nearly every one of these skimmers samples at 192 kHz, judging by the 130 to 150 kHz of each band it covers in CW and RTTY together; and those whose centers can be bracketed sit at the same place in both groups. The segment settings do not separate them either, since all of them decode beyond the non-contest segments. The evidence rules out a simple mismatch between the sample rate delivered and the rate the program assumes; it does not rule out other differences in driver versions or configuration. With one public build of the program since 2018, some station-specific setting or option presumably selects the difference, but the spots cannot reveal which one. Only the operators' settings can settle it.

    What needs to be determined is therefore twofold: what the members of each group have in common with one another, and what differs between the two groups. Everything the spots and the operators' public descriptions show is shared across the divide: the same program, sampling at 192 kHz, centers near the agreement points, decoding beyond the non-contest segments, and receivers of every kind on both sides. The difference must lie in something none of these sources records, such as a setting in the program's configuration, the driver and its version, or the computer the program runs on. A comparison of the configuration files and driver versions from a few stations in each group, the positive-slope group's above all, since it carries most of the stretch, would likely identify it.

    C.4 · What It Means for This Document

    None of this affects most of the operators who use the spots in a contest. The RBN's own telnet feed carries the 10 Hz frequencies, writing every frequency from every skimmer with two decimals, but users are discouraged from connecting to it directly, and most receive their spots through the clusters at 100 Hz resolution. For them the 10 Hz digits, the piles within each 100 Hz, and the mixed resolutions above never arrive; they matter to the few who take the RBN feed directly and to an analysis of the archive like this one, which they can confound. The ramp itself survives the rounding, but at a few tens of hertz it is far less than the 170 Hz shift of an RTTY signal and makes no difference to an operator tuning to a spot.

    Nothing in the main findings depends on it either. These offsets are smaller than a 100 Hz cell width, but they can move a report across a cell boundary, occasionally splitting a station between two adjacent cells. It is a reminder, though, of the limit stated in § 19: a spot carries a frequency but nothing about how it was computed, and two groups of carefully run skimmers can each be internally consistent and still disagree by 90 Hz; only the stations' own choice of frequencies and radios independent of the RBN showed which of them reads true. Precision is easy; accuracy takes a reference.

    Appendix D · A Decade of RTTY Improvement, and the Weekend That Broke It

    Everything above compares the Makrothen weekend with the weeks around it. That leaves a question open: was the network already drifting, so that Makrothen was a bad weekend for a network that had always been noisy, or was it a departure? To answer it, I applied exactly the method of this document, the four-skimmer consensus, the mechanism split of Table 4, and the clock correction of § 9, to three benchmark RTTY contests in every year the RBN archive allows: the ARRL RTTY Roundup (first full weekend of January, from 1800Z Saturday), the CQ WPX RTTY contest (second full weekend of February), and the CQ WW RTTY contest (last full weekend of September). The archive carries RTTY only from late 2012, and through 2014 there were too few RTTY skimmers, 8 to 22, for the consensus to judge much of anything, so the series starts in 2015. Each weekend was measured twice, as reported and with every skimmer whose offsets fit a clock error corrected by its own figure; the corrected figures are used throughout. The correction lowers the network rate by up to about a point in a year (CQ WW RTTY 2019), but it does not change the trend.

    D.1 · Ten Years of Steady Improvement

    Over those 10 years the network got markedly better (Table 11 and Figure 11). The median skimmer's off-consensus rate fell by about half, from between 5.9 and 9.6 percent of judged spots in 2015 and 2016 (above 6.4 in five of the six contests) to between 3.8 and 5.1 percent over 2023–2025. The spread narrowed even more: the upper quartile fell from between 8.2 and 11.6 percent in 2015 and 2016 to between 4.5 and 6.3 percent over 2023–2025. Not every year was better than the last. CQ WW RTTY sat between 6.3 and 7.2 percent from 2017 to 2020, above its own 2016 figure, CQ WPX RTTY was worse in 2016 than in 2015, a few bad skimmers lifted CQ WW RTTY's network rate in 2023 without moving its median, and CQ WW RTTY 2026 was slightly worse than 2025. But the direction over the decade is unmistakable, in all three contests.

    The improvement was concentrated in exactly the mechanism this document is about. In 2015 and 2016, callers reported as though they held the frequency were 2.2 to 4.3 percent of judged spots, the largest single kind of off-consensus spot in five of the six contests; from 2023 to 2025 they were 0.66 to 0.96 percent, apart from CQ WW RTTY 2023 at 2.1, the year a few bad skimmers lifted that contest's network figures. Garbles moved the other way, from about 1 percent to about 2 (3.1 to 3.3 at the Roundup in 2025 and 2026), and runners reported on the wrong frequency peaked between 2018 and 2021 at 3 to 4 percent in CQ WPX and CQ WW RTTY before settling at about 2 to 3 percent there and 1.3 at the Roundup. By 2025 a caller misattributed as a runner had become one of the rarest things an RTTY skimmer did wrong.

    D.2 · Why It Improved, as Far as the Spots Show

    The spots cannot say why, but they show what changed alongside. The network grew denser: the median number of skimmers backing a decided cell rose from 4 or 5 to 6 or 7, and the share of RTTY spots that could be judged at all rose from between 21 and 51 percent in 2015 to about 75 percent, so part of the decline is the measure itself growing surer. Receivers were better calibrated: the median skimmer's clock error, 1.4 to 2.2 ppm from 2018 to 2020, has been 0.7 to 0.9 ppm since 2023 except at CQ WW RTTY 2025 (1.3), with fewer skimmers 10 ppm or more off. It was not a skimmer upgrade: RTTY Skimmer Server, behind most RTTY spots, has had no new public build since 2018 (C.3).

    D.3 · Against That Background

    Measured the same way, the Makrothen weekend stands far outside a decade of these contests (Table 12). The network as a whole was off-consensus on 18.4 percent of judged spots, against a median of 5.1 percent for the nine benchmark weekends of 2023 to 2025 (4.1 to 7.4) and about twice the 9.0 percent of the worst benchmark since 2015 (CQ WPX RTTY 2016). Its caller share, 10.5 percent of judged spots, was 13 times the recent median of 0.80 percent (0.66 to 2.10), and more than twice the worst of any benchmark since 2015, 4.3 percent (CQ WW RTTY 2015). The decade's improvement had been concentrated in exactly that number, and one weekend undid it.

    The rest of the network was on trend. Without the eight, Makrothen's figures were 4.6 percent off-consensus and 1.12 percent callers, squarely within recent years; so was the median skimmer, 4.7 percent with the eight included and 4.1 without them: as 8 of 39 ranked skimmers, the eight raised the median by only 0.6 points. (Removing clock errors brings VU2OY-9 over the 100 judged spots needed to rank, so 39 skimmers rank here where § 3, measuring the spots as reported, ranks 38, with a median of 4.8 percent.) What they moved was the upper quartile, 20.2 percent against 4.5–6.3 for the recent benchmarks. A week later at JARL, with the defect still on several of the newer skimmers for part of the contest (§ 15), the network was off-consensus on 6.5 percent of judged spots, with callers at 2.6 percent, and by the Russian WW Multimode contest the UberSDR Skimmer roster's callers were back at or below the reference skimmers' (§ 15).

    That is what makes Makrothen more than a bad weekend. A network that had spent 10 years steadily learning not to report callers as runners, through more skimmers and better clocks, lost all of that ground in one contest, to eight skimmers on new software, and regained it only when the software was fixed. The defect's significance lies as much in what it undid as in what it did.

    Appendix E · The CW Record: No Comparable Break

    The measurement of Appendix D, applied to three benchmark CW contests, ARRL DX CW (third full weekend of February), CQ WPX CW (last full weekend of May), and CQ WW CW (last full weekend of November), gives the comparison the RTTY record needs (Table 13 and Figure 12). The archive reaches back to 2009 for CW, but through 2010 there were too few skimmers and too little could be judged, so the series starts in 2011; by then there were 75 to 96 CW skimmers in each contest, and there are some 240 now.

    CW shows no trend of the kind RTTY does. A single least-squares line through all three contests falls by 0.018 points a year for callers and 0.023 for all off-consensus spots, against 0.23 and 0.18 for RTTY, so CW has been essentially flat. The median skimmer was off-consensus on 5.2 percent of judged spots across 2011 to 2020 (the median of those contests) and 4.4 percent across 2022 to 2025. Callers reported as runners were never a large part of CW's errors: a median of 1.0 percent of judged spots over the whole period, 0.83 to 1.12 percent from 2022 to 2025, with no trend. CW skimmers appear to have told callers from runners reliably all along, while RTTY skimmers took a decade to learn to do so.

    Underneath the flat total the mix changed much as it did in RTTY. Runners reported on the wrong frequency fell from a median of 3.0 percent of judged spots over 2016–2020 to 1.9 over 2023–2025, and garbles rose from 1.2 to 2.0; garbles roughly doubled in RTTY over the same years. Calibration improved even more than in RTTY: the median skimmer's clock error, 1.0 to 2.1 ppm from 2011 to 2020, has been 0.4 to 0.7 ppm since 2023, and the clock correction that once lowered a contest's network rate by up to 2.5 points now lowers it by at most 1.0.

    Measured the same way, the Makrothen weekend's CW (both UTC days, as in § 11) was on trend: the network was off-consensus on 4.85 percent of judged spots, within the 4.4 to 5.3 percent of the benchmarks of 2022 to 2025. Its caller share, 1.38 percent, sat a little above the benchmarks' 0.83 to 1.12 percent, but was still 1.32 percent without the eight, so the eight are not the reason.

    However large or small the increase in off-consensus spots at the UberSDR Skimmer instances, in CW its effect on the network's statistics is diluted to the point of invisibility. The CW excess of § 11 is real skimmer by skimmer, but over the Makrothen weekend the eight were 8 of some 245 CW skimmers and sent 5.9 percent of the CW spots (§ 11.2): leaving them out moves the network's off-consensus rate by 0.15 points, from 4.85 to 4.70 percent, and its caller share by 0.06, from 1.38 to 1.32, well inside the scatter between one benchmark contest and the next. In RTTY the same eight were 8 of 43 skimmers and sent a third of the spots, and leaving them out moves the network rate by 13.7 points (calculated from unrounded rates), from 18.4 to 4.6 percent, and the caller share by 9.3, from 10.5 to 1.1. It is the same contrast § 11.2 draws, a small disturbance in a deep pool and a large one in a shallow pool, now set against a decade or more of each.

    That makes the RTTY record the sharper: in CW, callers spotted as runners were always rare and stayed rare; in RTTY they had become rare, and at Makrothen were suddenly more than 10 times the norm (Appendix D).

    Appendix F · Misattributed Callers on a Busy CW Band

    How many misattributed callers does a busy band produce with no software defect involved, and how does that number depend on activity? Anecdotal evidence cannot answer it: an operator who notices more caller spots on the bandmap may be seeing a defect or may only be seeing a busy contest. To measure it I took the first 10 hours of the 2026 ARRL November Sweepstakes CW, from 2100Z November 7 to 0700Z November 8, after which run rates fall off sharply, and the quiet days of November 3 to 6 for comparison. The 10-hour window spans two UTC days, November 7 and 8; the consensus for each of those two days, which decides who holds each frequency and whether a call was heard elsewhere that day, is built from all of that day's spots, but only cells inside the 10-hour window are counted, as in § 11.3. The quiet days are taken whole. A caller is defined as in Table 7; beacon frequencies are excluded and clock errors are not removed. Skimmers on the UberSDR Skimmer roster (the roster below) are counted separately from all others, which are overwhelmingly VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server (Appendix G.2).

    F.1 · By How Loud the Runner Is

    A runner's loudness is the number of distinct skimmers backing its decided cell, a five-minute slot on one 100 Hz frequency. For a loud runner, one backed by 30 to 79 skimmers, at least one skimmer outside the roster reported a misattributed caller in 12.5 to 12.9 percent of its slots during the Sweepstakes peak, against 6.5 to 7.0 percent on the quiet days (Table 14). With 12 slots an hour, that is about 1.5 slots an hour of running at the peak, against about 0.8 on the quiet days. The share stops rising once about 20 skimmers back the runner, because the caller rate per spot falls as the runner gets louder, from 1.66 percent to 0.24: the extra skimmers mostly report the runner itself, and the number of slots with a caller spot does not grow with them. A model that treats each skimmer's 0.66 percent median caller rate (Appendix G.1) as an independent chance per spot therefore overstates a loud runner's share by roughly two to four times. The roster's skimmers, about 8 percent of CW skimmers, put a caller into 10.9 to 11.6 percent of a loud runner's slots at the peak, nearly as often as all the other skimmers together and two to three times their own quiet-day share.

    F.3 · An Innocent Slip, Faithfully Reported

    "For our policemen, we created a race of robots. … we have given them absolute power over us. This power cannot be revoked."

    Klaatu, The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951)

    A skimmer has to decide from the decoded text alone which callsign is calling CQ. The simplest workable rule is that the call right after a CQ or TEST is the station calling CQ, and that a CQ belongs only to the first call after it, so a caller answering on the runner's own frequency is passed over. When a caller answers zero beat, the decoder hears both stations as one stream of text:

    Runner K1MKCQ SS K1MK Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    Read as "CQ SS K1MK W0XYZ", the CQ goes to K1MK, the first call after it, and W0XYZ is passed over as a caller. OpenSkimmer, Karl K5KHK's open-source skimmer (MIT license) and one of two CW skimmers whose interpretation code is public, the other being SparkGap (below), uses that rule with two guards: a TEST right after a call the skimmer already knows on that signal belongs to that call, and on a frequency it already credits to one station a new call needs its CQ immediately in front of it (callsign.py). VE3NEA has not published Skimmer Server's rules; this one serves here as a plain, reasonable baseline.

    Hypothesis, requiring confirmation: two ordinary operating slips, with no defect anywhere, can defeat that rule. A caller who presses the key for the CQ message (F1 in N1MM's default layout) instead of the one for their own call (F4), and stops it a moment too late, puts a stray CQ on the runner's frequency just ahead of their call:

    Runner K1MKCQ SS K1MK Caller W0XYZCQ (stopped) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    A runner whose automatic CQ repeat starts just as a caller answers, and who stops it, leaves the same kind of fragment, either a CQ after its own call or a CQ cut off before it:

    Runner K1MKTU K1MK CQ (stopped) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ
    Runner K1MKCQ SS (stopped) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    Nor need the slip be the misattributed caller's own. A stray CQ from a second caller on the frequency credits whichever call is decoded next, so an innocent third caller can be spotted as calling CQ for another station's mistake:

    Runner K1MKCQ SS K1MK Second callerCQ (stopped) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    Nor need that second station be zero beat. A skimmer copies each signal in a narrow channel, about 47 Hz wide in OpenSkimmer, and a station calling a little off frequency, or loud enough for its signal to bleed into the channel copying the runner, lands in the same stream of text. Its stray CQ then sits in front of whichever call that channel decodes next.

    Either way the rule does exactly what it says. The word in front of W0XYZ is a CQ, with no other call between them, so W0XYZ is credited with calling CQ. Neither guard applies: the first covers a TEST, not a CQ, and the second is satisfied because nothing at all stands between the CQ and the call. Once given, the credit stays with that call, so its next clean copy, such as the caller's own call in the Sweepstakes exchange, completes the spot. And because the stray CQ was transmitted, every skimmer applying such a rule to that frequency reads the same text: several can report the same misattributed caller at the same moment, however strong or weak the signals.

    A third case needs no slip at all. A runner usually sends its call only once after the CQ. If noise, a fade, or another station's signal turns that call into something that is not a callsign, the first caller decoded cleanly after it becomes the first call after the CQ:

    Runner K1MKCQ SS KJMK (K1MK, its 1 decoded as J, the final dah lost) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    The two open-source CW skimmers handle this differently: OpenSkimmer, and SparkGap, a multiband Linux skimmer for CW, RTTY, and FT8 (GPL-3.0 license) in daily use at WF8Z, whose spotting rules are likewise public. In OpenSkimmer a garbled word that is neither a callsign nor an unreadable "?" does not stop the search for the call after the CQ, so W0XYZ is credited with calling CQ unless that skimmer has already established K1MK on the signal, by spotting it or copying it cleanly twice; once credited, the caller's next clean copy completes the spot. SparkGap names W0XYZ too but is unlikely to spot it (spot_rule.py). Its exchange test, which treats a call followed by a report and a number as a caller being sent the exchange, would catch the case in a contest such as CQ WW but not in the Sweepstakes, whose exchange has no report and begins with a serial number. What protects the Sweepstakes example is the rest: while K1MK owns the frequency, a CQ naming another call in the same decode window does not count, and the runner's own call is in that window whenever its exchange is, since the Sweepstakes exchange includes it; and a call needs two to four separate decode windows within 10 minutes, so one such event is not enough. Its guard against a garbled runner's call, which would block the case outright, is off by default.

    If this case is common, a skimmer should misattribute callers mostly when it is not copying the runner. Table 16 tests that with each skimmer's own spots: for every misattributed caller in the samples of F.1, it asks whether the same skimmer had itself spotted the runner, within 300 Hz, in the 10 minutes before, only in the 10 minutes after, or in neither. VE3NEA's Skimmer Server spots a station at most once in 10 minutes, so a skimmer copying a runner that keeps running would spot it at least once in that 20-minute span; no spot of the runner there means the skimmer was not copying the runner's call.

It reports callers as runners on frequencies where it already knows who is running, which points to a missing guard in the program rather than to Rotten QRM. Two cautions apply. A skimmer's own spots only stand in for what it holds in memory: it can be copying the runner without a recent spot, since a call must be validated before it is spotted and is spotted at most once in 10 minutes, or it can have lost the runner despite one, since SparkGap forgets a frequency's owner after 3 minutes and OpenSkimmer resets a channel after 20 seconds without keying. And there is no baseline for how often a skimmer would have spotted the runner anyway. The contrast between groups in the same periods is the robust part; SparkGap's 172 and 34 spots are too few to judge.

    A public case may be one of these. During the 2026 ARRL November Sweepstakes CW, in the RBN-OPS thread "Told you so," Rudy N2WQ posted a screenshot of skimmers reporting VA3EC as calling CQ (RBN-OPS #9562) and noted that VA3EC had made 100 contacts at the time, against 10 such spots (#9589). In the RBN archive VA3EC was spotted 16 times by 13 skimmers from 2138Z November 7 to 0330Z November 8, on 12 occasions, each on the frequency of a different running station; 10 of the spots came from skimmers outside the UberSDR Skimmer roster. Misattributions spread over 12 runners rule out a single runner's habit, but not whose slip it was: VA3EC's own, or another caller's on the same frequency. The archive cannot show whether a CQ leaked, or from whom.

    Nothing in that chain is a defect. There was "Rotten QRM"1 (as there has been for nearly 110 years; F.4 measures it), or an operator made a slip every contester makes, or the decoder misread one word, as every decoder sometimes will, since none decodes without error; the rule was then applied as written, and the Aggregator forwarded a spot labeled CQ, as it should. No rule that reads text alone can tell a leaked CQ from a real one: a CQ message that goes out whole ("CQ SS W0XYZ") is, in the text, exactly what it says. And once the spot is sent, nothing recalls it. The RBN has no way to withdraw a spot, and a bandmap keeps it for its whole timeout. That is the bargain the network makes: judgment is handed to machines that apply a rule faithfully, in milliseconds, and cannot be overruled afterward. Nearly always that is exactly right. Here a burst of QRM, a slipped key, or one misread word, each lasting a fraction of a second, becomes a published spot that no one can take back.

    A lot of stuff happens during a contest. A busy run frequency is a mildly chaotic place: keys slip, macros fire and are cut off, and several callers pile onto one frequency at once. Seen against that, it is remarkable that skimmers reading nothing but decoded text, by rules as plain and simple as these, keep misattributed callers to fewer than 1 in 100 judged spots: a median of 0.66 percent for skimmers not running UberSDR Skimmer (Appendix G.1).

    The RBN archive records spots, not the decoded text behind them, so it cannot show how often this happens; recordings of a contest band, replayed through a skimmer, could. The rule is OpenSkimmer's, which decodes CW only; how VE3NEA's Skimmer Server or UberSDR Skimmer treats the same text, only their unpublished code can say. F.4 finds that misattributions do rise when another station stays on the runner's frequency.

    Appendix G · Looking for Additional Groups

    This appendix holds the detail behind § 11.4: the search for additional groups of skimmers with a CW excess, what the RBN's node list can and cannot say about the software and settings behind each skimmer, and the regional excess that turned out to be propagation.

    G.1 · Software Groups

    Is UberSDR Skimmer the only group of skimmers with a CW excess? The test is the one that found it (§ 10): join PSK Reporter's software table to each skimmer's CW rate. Over November 1 to 7, the seven days that table describes, 237 skimmers had at least 1000 judged CW spots, each with its receiver clock error removed (§ 9), and their median off-consensus rate was 3.41 percent. Of the 11 programs listed for at least three of them, only UberSDR Skimmer differs from the rest: its 26 skimmers ran a median of 6.57 percent against 3.37 for all the others, and callers at 2.17 percent against 0.66, a difference far past what testing 11 groups at once requires (Mann–Whitney p of 5 × 10−8, against a threshold of 0.0045). The UberSDR platform itself, whose operators skim CW with VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server, ran at 3.39 percent, and no other program differed from the rest by more than about half a point.

    That search is only as good as the software labels, and they are thin. Of the 237 skimmers, 137 do not report to PSK Reporter at all, and for many that do, the table names the bridge that forwards their spots, CW reporter, not the skimmer behind it. The RBN's own detailed node list, which records a version string for every connected skimmer, cannot fill the gap. Of the 259 nodes it listed on November 9, 217 reported skimmer version v.1.6.0.145, the version of VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server, and so did every one of the 19 UberSDR Skimmer nodes on it: a program that attempts to duplicate Skimmer Server's interface reports Skimmer Server's version. (An UberSDR Skimmer release on November 8 changed this for nodes running that software; by November 12, 14 nodes reported UberSDR Skimmer and its release instead.) On November 9 only five nodes named their program at all (SparkGap 1.2 on three, Wave Owl 1.17.0 and Hammerhead 0.1.0 on one each); by November 12, with UberSDR Skimmer's 14, there were 19. The four Meridian stations stand out only because they send the same digits without the "v.". Other than UberSDR Skimmer, which reports itself to PSK Reporter by name, a group of skimmers sending Skimmer Server's version string cannot be told from Skimmer Server itself by any public record, so the absence of another identifiable group is largely the absence of the means to identify one.

    G.2 · What the RBN Node List Records

    The node list records two settings each skimmer runs with, both reported to a SKIMMER/SETT query: its Validation Level, which sets how strictly a decoded callsign must pass the program's checks (watch list, Master.dta, callsign pattern, ITU block) before it is spotted, from Minimal through Normal and Aggressive to Paranoid, with "the more callsigns are rejected" the higher the level (CW Skimmer manual), and a spot filter that sends either all spots or CQ spots only. How many clean decodes a callsign needs before it is spotted depends on the level and on whether the call is on the operator's watch list or in the program's pattern file of known callsign patterns (Table 18): at Normal, a call the pattern file does not know must be decoded five times, at Minimal twice, and at Aggressive it is never spotted. Joining the list of November 9 to the same CW rates (206 of the 237 skimmers appear on it) shows neither setting separating the callers (Tables 19 and 20). The filter makes no difference in any category; skimmers set to send CQ spots only report callers at nearly the same rate as the rest, 0.63 against 0.73 percent. A caller misattributed as a runner is still sent as a CQ spot and passes the filter, consistent with the defect lying in the skimmer's interpretation rather than its decoding. All 19 UberSDR Skimmer nodes report the same settings, Normal Validation Level and all spots; the 113 other skimmers with those settings share 0.69 percent callers, so the settings do not explain the UberSDR Skimmer roster's excess either. Validation Level shows up in decoding instead, as its purpose suggests: the 16 skimmers at Minimal, the most lenient level, run 4.17 percent off-consensus against 3.34 for the 168 at Normal, mostly garbles, 1.77 against 1.21 percent (Mann–Whitney p of 1 × 10−6), while their callers differ only marginally (p of 0.04, above the 0.01 that testing five mechanisms at once requires). The three at Aggressive, which rejects more callsigns, have the fewest garbles, 0.38 percent. Both fit Table 18: a misdecode that no known pattern matches needs two clean decodes at Minimal rather than five, and is never spotted at Aggressive. Whether the Minimal level itself admits the garbles, or something else those 16 stations share, the spots cannot say.

    G.3 · SparkGap and OpenSkimmer

    Two other skimming programs come into the search, and neither can be tested as a group: one is named by only three nodes, the other by none. SparkGap 1.2 is the program those three nodes name on the RBN node list (Appendix G.2); those three, two of them one operator's, ran a median of 6.0 percent off-consensus with callers at 1.5: suggestive, but too few to call a group.

    OpenSkimmer is an open-source CW skimmer that Karl K5KHK published on November 10. Karl's own node, K5KHK, stands out: § 11.1 finds it above the UberSDR Skimmer group in CW over the Makrothen weekend with no clock error. It appears on the RBN node list as Skimmer Server v.1.6.0.145 and is not in PSK Reporter's software table, so the public records do not name its program. Before September 2026 it scarcely appears in the archive: 4 CW spots in CQ WW CW 2024, none in the 2025 benchmark contests, and 28 on August 15. From October 10 to 18 it ran 16 to 50 percent off-consensus in CW, with unconfirmed calls at 5 to 11 percent of its judged spots; over October 19 to 22 it fell to about 5 percent, the network median, and has stayed there. Whether the node ran OpenSkimmer, the RBN archive cannot definitively say, but the node was very likely running OpenSkimmer in development.

    G.4 · An Excess Shared by Location, Not Program: Asia

    The 18 skimmers in Asia ran a median off-consensus rate of 9.5 percent and a median caller rate of 5.4 percent, against 3.2 and 0.6 percent for the 126 in Europe and 3.5 and 0.8 percent for the 80 in North America. That excess is simply an artifact of propagation combined with frequency reuse. More than half of their off-consensus spots name an Asian station on a frequency the consensus gives to a European or North American station, and in most of those cases no Asian skimmer heard that station: two stations on the same frequency at once, each heard only where its signal reaches, while the consensus is decided by the far more numerous skimmers of Europe and North America. The Asian skimmers report what they hear correctly; the method scores them against stations they cannot hear (§ 19).

    The 40 m band shows it most plainly. During daylight hours in Japan it is effectively a different band from 40 m in Europe and North America at the same hour, and vice versa. Over November 3 to 5 the Japanese skimmers were off-consensus on 48.5 percent of their judged 40 m spots between 0800 and 1700 local time (2300 to 0800Z), and 35.2 percent of those judged spots named an Asian station on a frequency held by a European or North American station that no Asian skimmer reported; during nighttime hours in Japan the same skimmers were off-consensus on 13.7 percent of their judged 40 m spots, and 8.1 percent of those judged spots were of that kind. On the higher bands, where all regions share the same propagation more often, the effect fades: on 20 m in daylight hours in Japan the figures were 7.8 percent off-consensus and 2.9 of that kind, and 15 m almost nothing. In daylight hours in Japan, 40 m alone accounts for 62 percent of all such spots.
