Eight receivers that reported the caller, not the runner

On the Makrothen RTTY weekend, eight RBN skimmers reported the station answering a CQ at the frequency of the station calling it — on 42 to 50 percent of the spots where their output could be checked, against a 4.8 percent median across all receivers. All eight run the same skimmer program, and every receiver in the field that runs it is one of the eight. The same effect is present in their CW output at about a fifth the strength, and because CW is spotted every day it dates each node's change to a staggered 2–6 October and confirms the behavior was still running on 13 October.

10–11 October 2026 · 195,068 RTTY spots · prepared by K1MK

The callsigns are correct. Between 90 and 97 percent of the disputed calls are independently confirmed by four or more other receivers, and every one is a well-formed callsign. These receivers decode accurately.

What is wrong is the frequency's meaning. A spot says "this station is workable here". During a pile-up that is true of the runner and false of everyone calling it. These eight receivers report the callers.

It is new, and it arrived with RTTY itself. Two of the eight were measured two weeks earlier in CQ WW RTTY at 5.4 percent on roughly 32,000 checkable spots each. Three more were on the air that weekend producing 3,872 to 18,625 CW spots and not one RTTY spot. Their CW decoders were working; RTTY published nothing.

And it is five operators, not eight receivers. Rich K1RA runs three of the eight instances and Nathan M9PSY runs two, so the number of independent changes behind this is five.

The common factor is the software, and it partitions the field exactly. PSK Reporter lists nine stations running UberSDR Skimmer. Eight produced measurable RTTY on this weekend, and those eight are precisely the eight receivers above. No receiver running anything else exceeds 21.5 percent, and no control runs it.

How a spot is judged

Group every RTTY spot by band, frequency to 0.1 kHz, and a five-minute slot. Within each cell, the callsign that the most distinct receivers report is taken as the station holding the frequency. Where at least four receivers back that callsign, the cell is decided, and any spot in it naming a different station is counted as mis-attributed.

On this weekend 15,245 cells reached that threshold. Rates below are expressed over judged spots — those landing in a decided cell — not over a receiver's total output.

The denominator decides the answer, so it is stated everywhere. Dividing instead by total spots understates any receiver that rarely lands in a decided cell. In CQ WW RTTY, VU2OY-9 reads 5.6 percent that way (581 of 10,423) and 85.8 percent over judged spots (581 of 677). Judged is the right denominator for deciding whether to trust a receiver, but it rests on a thin slice when a receiver is seldom judged — VU2OY-9 is judged on 6 percent of its own output. Neither figure is meaningful without the count beside it.

1 · The split is not a gradient

A faulty decoder would be expected to spread across a range. These do not. Eight receivers sit between 41.6 and 50.2 percent, the ninth-worst at 9.0 percent, and the bulk of the field near 4 percent. MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 differ by 2 points, as do K1RA and K1RA-4, and that resemblance understates the real grouping. Five of the eight instances belong to two operators: K1RA, K1RA-4 and KW4VA are all run by Rich K1RA, and MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 are both run by Nathan M9PSY. The eight instances are therefore five operators — KC2DAC, K3FEF, MM0ZBH, and those two — which is the count that matters, because a decoder is configured per operator and not per callsign.

They need not be co-sited, and the spot data says they probably are not: on identical band coverage the two K1RA instances overlap on only 23.4% of frequency-and-moment keys and the two M9PSY instances on 38.8%, against 0.8–19.5% for fifteen unrelated pairs the same weekend. Two receivers at different locations genuinely hear different things. What binds them is the operator, not the antenna — so the right reading of "eight at once" is five operators changed something, with one of them taking three instances with him.

2 · It is not one loud station

3V8LL, Tunisia, drew the weekend's largest pile-ups and was the first case noticed. It is not the cause. The eight receivers mis-attribute on 68 to 74 percent of every runner they hear, against 25 to 34 percent for the reference receivers, and no single runner accounts for more than 4 to 9 percent of any one receiver's errors. The behavior follows the receiver, not the station.

3 · It does not worsen with the pile-up

This is the result that rules out a decoder simply being swamped. If a crowded frequency were overwhelming these receivers, the rate should climb as the crowd grows. It does not move. The reference receivers, meanwhile, get steadily better as more receivers agree.

6 · What it is not

Not a frequency calibration error

An offset readout would drop a receiver's spots into the wrong frequency bin, where the consensus names a different station, producing this signature with nothing wrong at the decoder. Comparing each receiver's reported frequency for the same station, band and slot against the median of the others, every ranked receiver — flagged and reference alike — returns a median offset of 0.000 kHz. The flagged receivers do show 3.4 to 7.0 kHz of scatter against the reference receivers' 1.4 to 2.2, which is what decoding a different signal looks like rather than a shifted dial.

Not separable by signal strength

SNR carries no usable information here. Spots matching the consensus have a median of 24 dB over 111,448 observations; those that do not, 25 dB over 24,064. An SNR floor makes matters slightly worse up to 30 dB and only reduces the caller share at 40 dB, where it also discards 88 percent of correct spots.

Not visible in the archive's other fields

The RBN daily archive has carried a stable 13 columns since 2016 and holds no validity or quality flag. Of the three quality-adjacent fields, two are inert for RTTY: speed is a constant 45 baud, and mode reads CQ on 117,504 of 117,521 RTTY spots on 10 October, because the aggregator tags RTTY that way regardless of what was sent.

7 · The common factor is one skimmer program

PSK Reporter publishes the decoder software each reporting station used over the preceding seven days, a window that covers this contest. Joining that list against the measured rates answers the question the spot data cannot: these receivers have one thing in common, and it is not geography, antennas, or an operator.

Nine stations are listed as running UberSDR Skimmer. Eight of them produced enough RTTY this weekend to be judged, and those eight are exactly the eight receivers in section 1. The ninth, M9PSY, is Nathan's third callsign and posted no RTTY. The partition holds in both directions:

  • No false negatives. Every judged UberSDR Skimmer station is in the 41.6–50.2 percent group; the group's median is 44.3 percent.
  • No false positives. Every ranked receiver above 30 percent runs UberSDR Skimmer. The worst receiver on any other software is 21.5 percent, and the controls run unrelated programs — CW reporter, CWSL_DIGI, and VE3NEA's own RTTY Skimmer Server v.1.3.0.432.

UberSDR is an open-source, web-based HF SDR platform built on ka9q-radio (ubersdr.org). Its published skimming support is aimed at FT4, FT8 and WSPR, plus a driver for VE3NEA's Skim Server; a dedicated RTTY skimmer is not part of its documented feature set, which is consistent with RTTY decoding being recent. This report does not identify which part of the program is responsible. What the data supports is narrower and sufficient: the behavior tracks the program, not the site, the antenna, or the operator.

What this does not establish. The software list covers only the last seven days, so it describes what these stations run now, not what they ran during CQ WW RTTY three weeks earlier. It therefore cannot show whether K1RA and K1RA-4 — clean at 5.4 percent then — changed programs, changed versions, or changed a setting. The version string for UberSDR Skimmer is not published in that table either, so the eight cannot be confirmed to share one build.

A second, milder cluster

The same join explains the weaker outliers, which had looked unrelated. Four stations run Meridian v0.1.25, and all four read above the field median — LB9KJ-1 at 17.1 percent this weekend and 13.2 percent in CQ WW, VU24DX-1 at 19.4 and 9.0, VU2CPL-88 at 8.3 in CQ WW, and VU2OY-9, CQ WW's own 85.8 percent outlier. That is a different pattern from the UberSDR Skimmer group: persistently elevated across both weekends rather than suddenly broken in one. It is worth reporting because it was the cluster the denominator correction surfaced, and because two programs showing the same class of error suggests the underlying mistake — treating a decode at a frequency as the station holding it — is an easy one to make.

8 · The same effect in CW, about a fifth as strong

These receivers post CW as well as RTTY — 3,246 to 14,298 CW spots each over this weekend — so the same test can ask how far the defect reaches. It reaches CW. Every one of the eight is elevated, and the excess appeared between the two weekends exactly as it did in RTTY, while the controls did not move.

All eight sit above the highest control, on 2,696 to 10,965 judged CW spots each — K3FEF's 12.2 percent against a 3.8 percent control median is some twenty-seven standard errors out, so the elevation is not sampling noise. The controls' own rate moved by 0.1 points between weekends.

For scale, in the same two weekends the RTTY rate for these receivers went from 0.9× the field median to 9.2×. CW went from 1.2× to 2.3×. The effect is real in CW and roughly a fifth as pronounced.

Not an artifact of the frequency bin

CW callers often offset a few hundred hertz to be heard, which could place a caller outside the runner's own 0.1 kHz bin and hide it from this test — the obvious reason a genuine CW problem might look mild. Re-running at ±0.2 and ±0.5 kHz tolerance rules that out. Widening the bin raises everyone's rate, because it also lumps genuinely separate stations together, but it shrinks the gap rather than opening it: the UberSDR-to-control ratio falls from 2.3× to 1.9× to 1.4×. There are no hidden offset callers to find, and the narrow bin is the more discriminating measurement. On the CQ WW weekend the ratio is 1.2×, 1.0× and 0.9× at the three widths — no excess at any of them.

Dating the change, node by node

RBN carries CW every day, so unlike RTTY it can date the change. Eighteen days were measured — 12 September for a baseline, then 26 September through 11 October — with each node compared to the same day's control median, so that a busy contest Saturday (3 October carried 947,000 CW spots against roughly 350,000 on a weekday) cannot be mistaken for a change in one receiver. The controls held between 3.2 and 4.6 percent across the whole window with no trend.

Each node's own series was then split at the point that best separates it, weighting every day by its judged-spot count because a rate from 300 spots is far noisier than one from 11,000. There is no single changeover date. Four nodes transition cleanly, on four different days:

The two sharpest transitions are the best evidence for a released build rather than individual tinkering: K1RA-4 and MM0ZBH both step between 5 and 6 October, from +1.0 and +1.6 points to +7.4 each, on eleven clean days before and six elevated days after, with no overlap between the two segments. They belong to different operators.

But the four datable transitions span five days, and that spread carries its own information — it is what adoption of a new version looks like as operators update at their own pace, rather than a single flag flipping everywhere. The four undatable nodes fit the same shape: two of them (M9PSY-2, KW4VA) were off the air entirely for the fortnight before they appeared, and came up already affected, which is what a node started on an already-updated install would do.

This corrects something earlier in this report. Section 1 argues that five of the eight instances belong to two operators, so the number of independent events is five rather than eight. The dating shows that instances under one operator did not change together: Rich K1RA's K1RA-4 steps on 5–6 October while his KW4VA first appears on 10 October, and Nathan M9PSY's MM9PSY steps on 3–4 October while his M9PSY shows no step and his M9PSY-2 arrives on the 9th. The rollout is per-instance, not per-operator, so the true number of independent events lies somewhere between five and eight, not at five.

Binary search was the wrong instrument, and the record is kept here deliberately. Bisecting the group median converged on 2–3 October after four archive days. That boundary is an artifact: the median covers whichever nodes clear the judged floor each day, and K3FEF and M9PSY first appear on 3 October already elevated, which moves the median without any node changing — two of the four continuing nodes actually went down that day. Bisection also assumes one monotonic step, and there is none. The per-node scan of all eighteen days answered the question instead, for twelve extra archive days and about 41 MB.

What CW does not show

Two things that hold in RTTY fail here, and both matter.

  • The partition does not hold. In RTTY the eight occupied the entire top of the field and nothing else exceeded 21.5 percent. In CW they do not lead: OH2BBT at 52.1 percent, OK2RZ at 51.2, K5KHK at 34.0 and OK4YL at 30.5 are all worse, and none of them runs UberSDR Skimmer. CW has its own unrelated bad actors — K5KHK was also 41.1 percent on the earlier weekend — and the UberSDR group is merely elevated within a noisier field rather than defining its top.
  • The real-call test stops discriminating. In RTTY, 90 to 97 percent of the group's disputed calls were confirmed by four or more other receivers against 80 to 91 percent for controls. In CW both sit at 77 to 89 percent. Everyone's CW misses are mostly real callers, so that test no longer separates the groups — it only confirms the mechanism is caller confusion in both.

The useful consequence is for localizing the fault: because CW is affected too, this is not purely a broken RTTY decoder. Something shared across modes changed. But the amplitudes differ by roughly fivefold, so a single shared cause and two separate ones fit the data equally well, and this report cannot choose between them.

The cost is much smaller than the spot count suggests

On the Makrothen weekend these nine nodes produced 69,277 of 195,068 RTTY spots, so excluding them discards 35.5 percent of the stream — the figure that makes a blacklist look expensive. It is misleading, because most of what is discarded is either duplicated elsewhere or was never useful.

494 stations were reported only by these nine all weekend, a quarter of the 1,991 stations seen. But 456 of those 494 — 92.3 percent — never held a frequency anywhere, by the same four-skimmer consensus test: they were callers inside somebody else's pile-up, and a spot naming them was never workable. The genuine exclusive loss is 38 running stations, 5.5 percent of the 686 that ran. Of the spots that survive the exclusion, 97.8 percent name a station that ran somewhere.

Multiplier risk for this weekend: two entities

JARL WW RTTY counts DXCC entities plus the call areas of W, VE, JA and VK, per band, so the question is not whether a station disappears but whether a multiplier does. Re-running the Makrothen weekend without these nine:

  • No W or VE call area is lost on any band. All 15 areas visible from runners stay visible on 80, 40, 20, 15 and 10 m. The 38 exclusive runners include 21 W/VE stations spread over W1–W5, W7, W9, W0 and VE3, and every one of those areas is covered by another runner.
  • Two DXCC entities are lost: DU (Philippines) on 40 m through DU4WL, and GU (Guernsey) on 15 m through GU0UVH. 72 of the 74 entities visible from runners survive.

So the trade is roughly 20,000 misleading spots removed against two rare entity multipliers that would need finding by tuning instead. On that balance the exclusion is worth taking for an assisted entry, and the recommendation here is to apply it to all nine — including M9PSY, which has no recent CW traffic to measure but runs the same program as the other eight.

These figures come from Makrothen, not from JARL — same nodes, same software state, four days earlier. Both are worked in every direction, which is what makes the proxy a reasonable one: JARL WW RTTY is a worldwide contest, and its multiplier structure treats JA, W, VE and VK symmetrically, each broken into call areas, rather than privileging any one of them. What differs is the scoring — Makrothen pays by distance with no multipliers at all, while JARL's multipliers are per band — so the two contests reward a different set of stations even when the same ones are audible. Expect the structure of the finding to carry over and the exact counts not to.

10 · Why the CT1BOH flags pass these spots

AR-Cluster's SKIMVALID is documented as being assigned "by looking back to see if there are two more spots of the same call, on the same frequency (± 0.3 kHz)". A caller inside a pile-up satisfies that by construction — the table in section 5 shows six receivers reporting one caller at one frequency within seconds. The spot is classified Valid, and correctly so: the callsign is real and it did transmit there.

SKIMCQ cannot separate them either, being a flag for the literal string "CQ" in the comment field, which RTTY spots carry indiscriminately. Both flags behave exactly as specified. Neither asks whether the spotted station is the one holding the frequency, which is the only question that distinguishes a caller from a runner.

11 · Scope and limits

  • Five operators, eight instances. Rich K1RA runs K1RA, K1RA-4 and KW4VA; Nathan M9PSY runs MM9PSY and M9PSY-2. Ownership comes from K1MK, not from the spot data, which cannot show it. It lowers the number of independent events from eight to five and raises the weight of each: three of Rich's instances behaving alike is one decision, not three.
  • "Absent" and "no RTTY" are different facts, and an RTTY-only spot count cannot tell them apart. Of the six not measurable in CQ WW RTTY, three (KC2DAC, MM9PSY, MM0ZBH) were in fact on the air spotting CW and published zero RTTY; only K3FEF, M9PSY-2 and KW4VA were absent entirely. An earlier draft of this report called all six "not on air", which was wrong and understated the case.
  • K1RA is the clearest evidence of a change, measured at 5.4 percent on ~32,000 judged spots in CQ WW and 45–48 percent two weeks later. The change cannot be dated more precisely: RTTY is contest-driven, there is almost none between those weekends, and Makrothen is the next RTTY contest after CQ WW, so there is no intermediate weekend to bisect.
  • CQ WW RTTY was not free of this. VU2OY-9 reached 85.8 percent there, on 677 judged spots, with CN3A at 24.7 and OK4ZW at 21.2. What distinguishes Makrothen is not that a receiver went wrong but that eight did at once, each on a large sample: at a 1,000-judged floor CQ WW's worst is 15.0 percent, 2.6× its median, while Makrothen still shows six receivers at 43–50 percent.
  • The weekends differ in size — 1,427,716 RTTY spots against 195,068 — so only the per-receiver comparison carries weight, never the aggregate difference between contests.
  • The common factor is established; the defect is not. Shared software explains which receivers misbehave, and the measurements in sections 2–6 constrain what kind of fault it is: flat in pile-up size, spread across nearly every runner, correct callsigns at correct frequencies. What the defect actually is, and which version introduced it, needs someone with the source — this report is intended to hand them a reproducible target.
