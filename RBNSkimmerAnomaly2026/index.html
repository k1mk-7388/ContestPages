Makrothen Skimmer Anomaly

Eight skimmers that over-reported the caller, not the runner

On the Makrothen RTTY weekend, eight RBN skimmers reported the station answering on the frequency of a running station — on 42 to 50 percent of the spots where their output could be checked, against a 4.8 percent median across all skimmers. All eight run the same skimmer program, and every skimmer in the field that runs it is one of the eight. The same effect is present in their CW output at roughly a quarter the strength, and because CW is spotted every day it dates each node's change to a staggered 2–6 October and confirms the behavior was still running on 14 October.

10–11 October 2026 · 195,068 RTTY spots · CW checked to 14 Oct · prepared by K1MK

Where this started

Nothing about the investigation reported here began with a rate. It began with an N1MM bandmap that looked wrong.

Below is ten minutes of 40 m RTTY, on the evening of 10 October. The spots have already been through an operator's own cluster filter and a check for mis-decoded callsigns, so nothing here is raw noise.

Three stations were running: WT2P on 7061.8, VE2BVV on 7062.5 and 3V8LL on 7065.2. Each is followed down the map by a fan of other callsigns on its own frequency — ten entries on 7065.2 alone.

Of the ten on 7065.2, only one — 3V8LL — was the station running there. The other nine were answering 3V8LL, and every one of them ran somewhere else that weekend, so they are real stations and nothing about the callsigns is wrong. An operator working down the bandmap makes one contact and then hits nine more bandmap entries that are all the same running station.

Ten stations typically do not occupy one frequency, so something was wrong. The rest of this report is the work of finding out what, and it ends up naming a specific piece of software. The short version: nine of those ten were answering 3V8LL rather than running, and a handful of skimmers were reporting them as though they were CQing on the frequency as well.

Callers being spotted on a runner's frequency is a well-known artifact and a fact of life (at a low level) — the report measures that background rate, and it is a few percent — which assisted stations deal with routinely. What made the Makrothen weekend different was the vastly increased extent.

How a spot is judged

The measurement below is RTTY only: every spot whose mode is anything else — CW, FT8, PSK — is discarded before the first grouping, not filtered afterwards. That matters more than it sounds, because the one measurement artifact found in this work (section 8) comes from the international beacon frequencies, which are CW.

Group every RTTY spot by band, frequency to 0.1 kHz, and a five-minute slot. Call each of those buckets a run frequency cell. Within each cell, the callsign that the most distinct skimmers report is taken as the station holding the frequency. Where at least four skimmers back that callsign, the run frequency cell is decided, and any spot in it naming a different station is counted as off-consensus.

Off-consensus is a measurement, not a verdict. A spot can be off-consensus for several unrelated reasons — a mis-decoded callsign, a station answering rather than running, a receiver whose frequency readout is offset, a frequency changing hands inside the slot — and section 6 separates them. Every skimmer in the field produces some: the controls run at 1.9 to 4.1 percent. The word mis-attribution is reserved here for the one mechanism this report is about — a correctly decoded callsign reported as though it held a frequency it was only answering on.

On this weekend 15,245 run frequency cells reached that threshold. Rates below are expressed over judged spots — those landing in a decided run frequency cell — not over a skimmer's total output.

The denominator decides the answer, so it is stated everywhere. Dividing instead by total spots understates any skimmer that rarely lands in a decided run frequency cell. In CQ WW RTTY, VU2OY-9 reads 5.6 percent that way (581 of 10,423) and 85.8 percent over judged spots (581 of 677). Judged is the right denominator for deciding whether to trust a skimmer, but it rests on a thin slice when a skimmer is seldom judged — VU2OY-9 is judged on 6 percent of its own output. Neither figure is meaningful without the count beside it.

1 · The split is not a gradient

A faulty decoder would be expected to spread across a range. These do not. Eight skimmers sit between 41.6 and 50.2 percent, the ninth-worst at 9.0 percent, and the bulk of the field near 4 percent. MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 differ by 2 points, as do K1RA and K1RA-4, and that resemblance understates the real grouping. Five of the eight instances belong to two operators: K1RA, K1RA-4 and KW4VA are all run by Andy K1RA, and MM9PSY and M9PSY-2 are both run by Nathan M9PSY. The eight instances are therefore five operators — KC2DAC, K3FEF, MM0ZBH, and those two — which is the count that matters, because a decoder is configured per operator and not per callsign.

They need not be co-sited, and the spot data says they probably are not: on identical band coverage the two K1RA instances overlap on only 23.4% of frequency-and-moment keys and the two M9PSY instances on 38.8%, against 0.8–19.5% for fifteen unrelated pairs the same weekend. Two skimmers at different locations genuinely hear different things. What binds them is the operator, not the antenna — so the right reading of "eight at once" is five operators changed something, with one of them taking three instances with him.

2 · It is not one loud station

3V8LL, Tunisia, drew the weekend's largest pile-ups and was the first case noticed. It is not the cause. The eight skimmers go off-consensus on 68 to 74 percent of every runner they hear, against 25 to 34 percent for the reference skimmers, and no single runner accounts for more than 4 to 9 percent of any one skimmer's errors. The behavior follows the skimmer, not the running station.

3 · It does not worsen with the pile-up

This is the result that rules out a decoder simply being swamped. If a crowded frequency were overwhelming these skimmers, the rate should climb as the crowd grows. It does not move. The reference skimmers, meanwhile, get steadily better as more skimmers agree.

6 · What it is not

Not a frequency calibration error — for these eight

An offset readout would drop a receiver's spots into the wrong frequency bin, where the consensus names a different station, producing this signature with nothing wrong at the decoder. Comparing each receiver's reported frequency for the same station, band and slot against the median of the others, every skimmer ranked on this weekend — flagged and reference alike — returns a median offset of 0.000 kHz. The flagged skimmers do show 3.4 to 7.0 kHz of scatter against the reference skimmers' 1.4 to 2.2, which is what decoding a different signal looks like rather than a shifted dial.

That control was run on the Makrothen weekend, and one station needed it on the other one. Manoj VU2CPL, co-author of the Meridian skimmer software, reproduced this report's method from the RBN archives and pointed out that VU2OY-9 — CQ WW's 85.8 percent outlier, cited twice in this report — is a receiver frequency offset rather than caller mis-attribution. Re-running the same control on the CQ WW days confirms it:

40 m  +100 Hz  ·  20 m  +200 Hz  ·  15 m  +300 Hz  ·  mean +13.7 ppm

The offset scales with band, which is the signature of a fractional error in the sampling clock rather than anything in the decoder, and it is identical on CW and RTTY — +100 Hz on 40 m and +300 Hz on 15 m in both — which no decoder fault would produce in two unrelated demodulators. At +300 Hz on 15 m a 0.1 kHz consensus bin places nearly every spot in a neighboring cell, so this method scores the station as off-consensus by construction. It also explains the oddity noted below, that only 677 of that station's 10,423 spots were judgeable at all. VU2CPL reports the cause as a calibration bug applying another receiver's correction constant, now fixed.

The lesson for the method is general: a per-skimmer frequency check has to run before any off-consensus rate is believed, and it has to run on the same days as the rate. A clock error of a few parts per million is indistinguishable from caller mis-attribution in the output, and distinguishable immediately in the frequencies.

Not mis-decoded callsigns

A off-consensus spot can carry a garbled version of the runner's own call rather than somebody else's — a single-character RTTY error, published because the runner's CQ preceded it. That is a decoder fault, not a frequency-attribution fault, and it is a different problem with a different fix. Classifying every off-consensus spot by its edit distance from the cell's consensus holder separates the two, a test proposed by VU2CPL and run here on both weekends:

The headline was never carried by decoder errors.

Not separable by signal strength

SNR carries no usable information here. Spots matching the consensus have a median of 24 dB over 111,448 observations; those that do not, 25 dB over 24,064. An SNR floor makes matters slightly worse up to 30 dB and only reduces the caller share at 40 dB, where it also discards 88 percent of correct spots.

Not visible in the archive's other fields

The RBN daily archive has carried a stable 13 columns since 2016 and holds no validity or quality flag. Of the three quality-adjacent fields, two are inert for RTTY: speed is a constant 45 baud, and mode reads CQ on 117,504 of 117,521 RTTY spots on 10 October, because the aggregator tags RTTY that way regardless of what was sent.

7 · The common factor is one skimmer program

PSK Reporter publishes the decoder software each reporting station used over the preceding seven days, a window that covers this contest. Joining that list against the measured rates answers the question the spot data cannot: these skimmers have one thing in common, and it is not geography, antennas, or an operator.

Nine stations were listed as running UberSDR Skimmer when this was first checked on 14 October. Eight of them produced enough RTTY on the Makrothen weekend to be judged, and those eight are exactly the eight skimmers in section 1. The ninth, M9PSY, is Nathan's third callsign and posted no RTTY. The partition holds in both directions:

A tenth, K1HTV-4, joined the roster on 15 October. It came on the air late on the 14th and produced 41 CW spots in a 28-minute window, 23 of them judged — far too thin to rank, and its first-pass reading turned out to be the beacon artifact described in section 8 rather than any sign of the defect. It is listed here because it runs the program, not because it has been measured.

  • No false negatives. Every judged UberSDR Skimmer station is in the 41.6–50.2 percent group; the group's median is 44.3 percent.
  • No false positives. Every ranked skimmer above 30 percent runs UberSDR Skimmer. The worst skimmer on any other software is 21.5 percent, and the controls run unrelated programs — CW reporter, CWSL_DIGI, and VE3NEA's own RTTY Skimmer Server v.1.3.0.432.

UberSDR is an open-source, web-based HF SDR platform built on ka9q-radio (ubersdr.org). Its published skimming support is aimed at FT4, FT8 and WSPR, plus a driver for VE3NEA's Skim Server; a dedicated RTTY skimmer is not part of its documented feature set, which is consistent with RTTY decoding being recent. This report does not identify which part of the program is responsible. What the data supports is narrower and sufficient: the behavior tracks the program, not the site, the antenna, or the operator.

What this does not establish. The software list covers only the last seven days, so it describes what these stations run now, not what they ran during CQ WW RTTY three weeks earlier. It therefore cannot show whether K1RA and K1RA-4 — clean at 5.4 percent then — changed programs, changed versions, or changed a setting. The version string for UberSDR Skimmer is not published in that table either, so the eight cannot be confirmed to share one build.

A second grouping, and a different mechanism

The same join surfaced four stations running Meridian v0.1.25 — LB9KJ-1, VU24DX-1, VU2CPL-88 and VU2OY-9 — all reading above the field median. An earlier version of this report treated them as a milder case of the same effect. They are not, and the correction is owed to Manoj VU2CPL, co-author of Meridian, who reproduced the method independently and went through the off-consensus spots against his stations' own records.

Two separate things were happening, neither of them what this report described:

  • VU2OY-9's 85.8 percent is the frequency offset described in section 6, not mis-attribution at all.
  • The rest is mostly garbled decodes of the runner's own callsign — 39 to 57 percent of their off-consensus spots, against 0 to 1 percent for the UberSDR Skimmer group. The two programs show different faults, not one shared conceptual error, and the sentence in an earlier version suggesting otherwise was wrong.

On the caller mechanism itself the Meridian stations sit between the controls and the UberSDR Skimmer group: 2.9 and 4.2 percent of judged spots in CQ WW, 7.6 and 9.8 percent over the Makrothen weekend, against 1.9 to 4.1 percent for controls and 40.5 to 48.6 percent for the eight. VU2CPL's own figures for his stations are lower again, at the reference level, and the gap is a definitional one worth stating rather than splitting: his classification draws on what his receivers actually recorded, while this one can only ask whether a callsign is corroborated elsewhere in the field, so spots he can place as unconfirmed are counted here as callers. Both measures put his stations far below the eight, which is the comparison that matters. He reports the garble leak as being closed in the next Meridian release.

One further station is worth the same treatment, and the suggestion is his: KM3T-5 reads 13.0 percent on the caller mechanism in CQ WW — above every Meridian station and the highest in that weekend's field — while falling to 2.1 percent over Makrothen. OK2PMU's excess splits more evenly: of its 10.3 percent off-consensus rate, 4.6 points are garbles against 5.6 points callers. Neither station was examined in this report before he pointed at them.

Taking KM3T-5 apart on one denominator, since two of its figures otherwise round to the same number for different reasons: over the CQ WW weekend it went off-consensus on 15.0 percent of its 29,062 judged spots, and that divides into 13.0 points of callers and 2.0 points of garbles. Its caller rate is the highest in that weekend's field; its garble rate is among the lowest. What distinguishes it is callers, not mis-copies.

Testing a weather explanation for KM3T-5

K1MK suggested a cause. The station is in south-eastern New Hampshire, and a major early-season nor'easter crossed the region over the CQ WW weekend with heavy rain and high winds. Three mechanisms could follow from that — precipitation static, lightning, and wind shaking antenna hardware or power-line fittings loose enough to arc — and each raises the noise a receiver has to decode through. A marginal runner lost in that noise while a louder caller still decodes would produce exactly this signature — callers, not garbles. Eight tests follow, each against the same regional skimmers (WZ7I, AA4PA, K9LC) so that anything common to the whole band drops out.

  • Did it stop hearing runners? For every judged run frequency cell a skimmer spotted into, the share in which it named the runner. KM3T-5 fell from 98.4 percent over Makrothen to 87.5 percent over CQ WW; its peers moved by 0.7 to 1.7 points. Measured as a difference-in-differences, that is a 10-point deficit specific to this station and this weekend — real, and the same event as its caller rate.
  • Was the loss geographic? If local noise let near, strong signals through and stopped far, weak ones, its off-consensus spots should over-represent US callers on DX runners. They do not: its share moved by 5.0 points against a peer median of 5.6. No geographic selectivity.
  • One receiver, or the site? A single faulty receiver would confine the loss to one band. It appears on all three of the station's active bands — 20 m, 15 m and 40 m — so it affected the station as a whole.
  • Does its reported SNR show it? No, and that reading should not be trusted either way. KM3T-5 reported a 27 dB median against a 22 dB field median. A skimmer measures SNR against its own noise floor, so a uniform rise is largely invisible; and only signals that decode are spotted at all, so a raised floor removes the weak ones and biases what survives upward.
  • Was the weather real, and does the deficit follow it? Hourly observations from three nearby ASOS stations — Nashua, Manchester and Portsmouth — show rain through most of the weekend and gusts to about 30 knots, as described. But the deficit does not follow rainfall intensity: some of the heaviest rain of the weekend coincided with normal reception at KM3T-5. Nashua's own gauge cannot be used here; it reported zero accumulation in all 45 observations in which its sensor was reporting rain.
  • Does it follow the wind? Arcing from loose hardware should track wind load rather than rain. No affected hour fell in calm conditions — every one came after the storm arrived, when gusts were 20 knots or more. But within the storm, gust strength did not predict which hours were affected: about half were, at every level from 20 to 32 knots, and four hours with gusts of 30 to 31 knots showed entirely normal reception. Direction cannot be tested, since the wind blew from the north-east almost the whole weekend, as a nor'easter's does.
  • Was there lightning nearby? Two sources were used. ASOS stations report thunder and automatically detected lightning out to about 30 nautical miles; surveys of 35 stations across six states and 28 along the storm's coastal track found lightning widespread on 26 September, from Long Island to Nantucket, and on 27 September only at three Jersey shore stations 362 km and more away. Land stations cannot see convection offshore, so the second source is the Geostationary Lightning Mapper on GOES-East, which maps every flash, in cloud and to ground, over land and sea, from NOAA's public archive. Within 1,500 km of the station it recorded 6,615 flashes on 26 September and 19,086 on the 27th. On 26 September the station's worst hours coincided with the storm's closest approach, the nearest flashes about 100 km away. But earlier that day, with as much lightning within 300 km and the nearest flash 145 to 243 km away, it heard normally. On 27 September it was affected for ten consecutive hours with at most one flash within 600 km in any of them, while hours earlier that day with as much or more lightning within 1,000 km were clean. Across the weekend, hourly flash counts and the deficit are uncorrelated at every radius tested (Spearman ρ between −0.05 and +0.17).
  • Did lightning arrive by skywave? K1MK pointed out that static from distant storms travels by the ionosphere, and so keeps propagation's schedule rather than the storm's: 20 m and 15 m carry it from beyond their skip zones while open, and 40 m from thousands of kilometers when it goes long. GLM's full view of the Americas and the Atlantic — 1.1 to 1.5 million flashes a day — was counted by hour in distance bands out past 6,000 km and compared per band. The Makrothen weekend is a poor control here: a severe geomagnetic storm (Kp 7) disturbed propagation through much of 10 October. The two CQ WW days are a good one: both were geomagnetically quiet (Kp 2.7 or below in the hours that matter) with similar solar flux, so the same UTC hour on each day had the same hop geometry. Matched that way, no band of distance tracks the deficit throughout. In the two hours where KM3T-5 heard normally on one day and was 44 to 68 points below its peers on the other, on 20 m and 15 m alike, the normal day had as much or more lightning at 2,000 to 4,500 km; only the 4,500 to 6,000 km band was heavier on the affected day. That band then fails the other way: in hours where it carried up to four times more lightning on 26 September, the deficit was deeper on the 27th. Lightning beyond 6,000 km differed as much as seventeen-fold between the days in hours with a similar deficit. And 40 m, with heavy lightning 2,000 to 6,000 km away on both nights, showed no deficit. The peers argue the same way: WZ7I, 393 km from KM3T-5 by the locators both report to PSK Reporter, would receive static from a storm thousands of kilometers away by nearly the same path, and was unaffected — though neither station's antenna directivity is known.

Read together: the storm was real, but lightning does not explain the deficit. It was tested nearby and by skywave, under matched propagation, and fits neither: the second day's deficit ran its course with no lightning in range, and no band of distance follows it from hour to hour across the two days. On the first day nearby lightning may have added to it, but as much lightning earlier that day left reception normal. The deficit also tracks neither rainfall nor gust strength: it never appeared in calm air, but storm-strength rain and wind often coincided with normal reception, which argues against a cause that simply scales with either. Precipitation static is weakened as the main explanation rather than excluded — its severity depends on the charge carried and the rate of impact, not simply on how much rain falls, and stations 20 to 65 km away are not the antenna site. The cause of KM3T-5's deficit is not established here. The station's own noise records would settle it where the spot archive cannot.

8 · The same effect in CW, roughly a quarter as strong

These skimmers post CW as well as RTTY — 3,246 to 14,298 CW spots each over this weekend — so the same test can ask how far the defect reaches. It reaches CW. Every one of the eight is elevated, and the excess appeared between the two weekends exactly as it did in RTTY, while the controls did not move.

Every CW figure here excludes the international beacon frequencies, and an earlier version of this report did not. The NCDXF/IARU beacon project puts eighteen beacons on one frequency per band — 14100.0, 18110.0, 21150.0, 24930.0 and 28200.0 kHz — each transmitting for ten seconds in rotation. A five-minute consensus slot can name only one holder, so every other beacon heard in that slot was scored as off-consensus while being entirely correct.

It was not a constant offset to subtract. It accounted for 65.3 percent of K3FEF's CW misses, 56.3 percent of MM0ZBH's and 55.6 percent of K1RA-4's, but 0.0 percent for four of the seven controls, which simply do not cover those frequencies. The published CW excesses were therefore inflated by roughly two to three times, and every number below is the corrected one. It was found by examining a newly-added node's first 23 spots by hand, which is an argument for reading individual spots and not only rates.

The RTTY analysis is unaffected, because the beacons are CW and the primary measurement discards every non-RTTY spot before it groups anything. Re-running sections 1–7 with these frequencies excluded moves the group median from 44.3 to 44.4 percent: 0.85 percent of all RTTY misses sit on an IBP frequency, at most 1.4 percent for any one node.

Not an artifact of the frequency bin

CW callers often offset a few hundred hertz to be heard, which could place a caller outside the runner's own 0.1 kHz bin and hide it from this test — the obvious reason a genuine CW problem might look mild. Re-running at ±0.2 and ±0.5 kHz tolerance rules that out. Widening the bin raises everyone's rate, because it also lumps genuinely separate stations together, but it shrinks the gap rather than opening it: the UberSDR-to-control ratio falls from 2.3× to 1.9× to 1.4×. There are no hidden offset callers to find, and the narrow bin is the more discriminating measurement. On the CQ WW weekend the ratio is 1.2×, 1.0× and 0.9× at the three widths — no excess at any of them.

Dating the change, node by node

RBN carries CW every day, so unlike RTTY it can date the change. Eighteen days were measured — 12 September for a baseline, then 26 September through 11 October — with each node compared to the same day's control median, so that a busy contest Saturday (3 October carried 947,000 CW spots against roughly 350,000 on a weekday) cannot be mistaken for a change in one skimmer. The controls held between 3.2 and 4.6 percent across the whole window with no trend.

Each node's own series was then split at the point that best separates it, weighting every day by its judged-spot count because a rate from 300 spots is far noisier than one from 11,000. There is no single changeover date. Four nodes transition cleanly, on four different days:

The four datable transitions now fall in two pairs — two on 2–3 October and two on 5–6 October — and that spread carries its own information — it is what adoption of a new version looks like as operators update at their own pace, rather than a single flag flipping everywhere. The four undatable nodes fit the same shape: two of them (M9PSY-2, KW4VA) were off the air entirely for the fortnight before they appeared, and came up already affected, which is what a node started on an already-updated install would do.

This corrects something earlier in this report. Section 1 argues that five of the eight instances belong to two operators, so the number of independent events is five rather than eight. The dating shows that instances under one operator did not change together: Andy K1RA's K1RA-4 steps on 5–6 October while his KW4VA first appears on 10 October, and Nathan M9PSY's MM9PSY steps on 3–4 October while his M9PSY shows no step and his M9PSY-2 arrives on the 9th. The rollout is per-instance, not per-operator, so the true number of independent events lies somewhere between five and eight, not at five.

Binary search was the wrong instrument, and the record is kept here deliberately. Bisecting the group median converged on 2–3 October after four archive days. That boundary is an artifact: the median covers whichever nodes clear the judged floor each day, and K3FEF and M9PSY first appear on 3 October already elevated, which moves the median without any node changing — two of the four continuing nodes actually went down that day. Bisection also assumes one monotonic step, and there is none. The per-node scan of all eighteen days answered the question instead, for twelve extra archive days and about 41 MB.

What CW does not show

Two things that hold in RTTY fail here, and both matter.

  • The partition does not hold. In RTTY the eight occupied the entire top of the field and nothing else exceeded 21.5 percent. In CW they do not lead: OH2BBT at 52.1 percent, OK2RZ at 51.2, K5KHK at 34.0 and OK4YL at 30.5 are all worse, and none of them runs UberSDR Skimmer. CW has its own unrelated bad actors — K5KHK was also 41.1 percent on the earlier weekend — and the UberSDR group is merely elevated within a noisier field rather than defining its top.
  • The real-call test stops discriminating. In RTTY, 90 to 97 percent of the group's off-consensus calls were confirmed by four or more other skimmers against 80 to 91 percent for controls. In CW both sit at 77 to 89 percent. Everyone's CW misses are mostly real callers, so that test no longer separates the groups — it only confirms the mechanism is caller confusion in both.

The useful consequence is for localizing the fault: because CW is affected too, this is not purely a broken RTTY decoder. Something shared across modes changed. But the amplitudes differ by roughly fivefold, so a single shared cause and two separate ones fit the data equally well, and this report cannot choose between them.

9 · Still happening, and what it costs to exclude them

The defect has not been fixed. Measured through 14 October — the latest complete archive day, beacon frequencies excluded — every node still transmitting is elevated and none has returned to its own baseline. The six run +2.9 to +4.1 points above the controls, which sat at 3.0 percent over the same days. A tenth node, K1HTV-4, came on the air on the 14th with too little traffic to rank.

Three of the nine are currently dark

Three nodes report no CW at all over 12–14 October, and that needed checking rather than assuming, because a mode-filtered count cannot tell a skimmer that was switched off from one that is running and producing something else — a distinction this investigation got wrong once already. They are genuinely off the air: K1RA, KW4VA and M9PSY show zero spots in any mode on all three days. K1RA and KW4VA were both up on the 11th with near-identical output — 1,321 spots each, about 1,300 of them CW — and went silent together from the 12th; both are Andy K1RA's, while his K1RA-4 stayed up and is still the second-worst of the group. M9PSY has been dark since the 9th.

This lowers what an exclusion currently costs, below the estimate in the next section: a third of the list is not transmitting at all, so excluding those three is a no-op while they stay down. They are still worth listing, because nothing suggests they were taken down to fix anything, and a node brought back up for a contest would come back on whatever it was running.

The cost is much smaller than the spot count suggests

On the Makrothen weekend these nine nodes produced 69,277 of 195,068 RTTY spots, so excluding them discards 35.5 percent of the stream — the figure that makes a blacklist look expensive. It is misleading, because most of what is discarded is either duplicated elsewhere or was never useful.

494 stations were reported only by these nine all weekend, a quarter of the 1,991 stations seen. But 456 of those 494 — 92.3 percent — never held a frequency anywhere, by the same four-skimmer consensus test: they were callers inside somebody else's pile-up, and a spot naming them was never workable. The genuine exclusive loss is 38 running stations, 5.5 percent of the 686 that ran. Of the spots that survive the exclusion, 97.8 percent name a station that ran somewhere.

Multiplier risk for this weekend: two entities

JARL WW RTTY counts DXCC entities plus the call areas of W, VE, JA and VK, per band, so the question is not whether a station disappears but whether a multiplier does. Re-running the Makrothen weekend without these nine:

  • No W or VE call area is lost on any band. All 15 areas visible from runners stay visible on 80, 40, 20, 15 and 10 m. The 38 exclusive runners include 21 W/VE stations spread over W1–W5, W7, W9, W0 and VE3, and every one of those areas is covered by another runner.
  • Two DXCC entities are lost: DU (Philippines) on 40 m through DU4WL, and GU (Guernsey) on 15 m through GU0UVH. 72 of the 74 entities visible from runners survive.

So the trade is roughly 20,000 misleading spots removed against two rare entity multipliers that would need finding by tuning instead. On that balance the exclusion is worth taking for an assisted entry, and the recommendation here is to apply it to all nine — including M9PSY, which has no recent CW traffic to measure but runs the same program as the other eight.

These figures come from Makrothen, not from JARL — same nodes, same software state, four days earlier. Both are worked in every direction, which is what makes the proxy a reasonable one: JARL WW RTTY is a worldwide contest, and its multiplier structure treats JA, W, VE and VK symmetrically, each broken into call areas, rather than privileging any one of them. What differs is the scoring — Makrothen pays by distance with no multipliers at all, while JARL's multipliers are per band — so the two contests reward a different set of stations even when the same ones are audible. Expect the structure of the finding to carry over and the exact counts not to.

10 · Why the CT1BOH flags pass these spots

AR-Cluster's SKIMVALID is documented as being assigned "by looking back to see if there are two more spots of the same call, on the same frequency (± 0.3 kHz)". A caller inside a pile-up satisfies that by construction — Table 3 shows six skimmers reporting one caller at one frequency within seconds. The spot is classified Valid, and correctly so: the callsign is real and it did transmit there.

SKIMCQ cannot separate them either, being a flag for the literal string "CQ" in the comment field, which RTTY spots carry indiscriminately. Both flags behave exactly as specified. Neither asks whether the spotted station is the one holding the frequency, which is the only question that distinguishes a caller from a runner.

What this amounts to

The callsigns are correct. Between 90 and 97 percent of the off-consensus calls are independently confirmed by four or more other skimmers, and every one is a well-formed callsign. These skimmers decode accurately.

What is wrong is the frequency's meaning. A spot says "this station is workable here". During a pile-up that is true of the runner and false of everyone calling it. Every skimmer reports a caller occasionally; these eight did it on nearly half their checkable spots.

It is new, and it arrived with RTTY itself. Two of the eight were measured two weeks earlier in CQ WW RTTY at 5.4 percent on roughly 32,000 checkable spots each. Three more were on the air that weekend producing 3,872 to 18,625 CW spots and not one RTTY spot. Their CW decoders were working; RTTY published nothing.

And it is five operators, not eight skimmers. Andy K1RA runs three of the eight instances and Nathan M9PSY runs two, so the number of independent changes behind this is five.

The common factor is the software, and it partitions the field exactly. PSK Reporter listed nine stations running UberSDR Skimmer on 14 October, and a tenth, K1HTV-4, appeared the next day. Eight produced measurable RTTY on this weekend, and those eight are precisely the eight skimmers above. No skimmer running anything else exceeds 21.5 percent, and no control runs it.

11 · Scope and limits

  • Five operators, eight instances. Andy K1RA runs K1RA, K1RA-4 and KW4VA; Nathan M9PSY runs MM9PSY and M9PSY-2. Ownership comes from K1MK, not from the spot data, which cannot show it. It lowers the number of independent events from eight to five and raises the weight of each: three of Andy's instances behaving alike is one decision, not three.
  • "Absent" and "no RTTY" are different facts, and an RTTY-only spot count cannot tell them apart. Of the six not measurable in CQ WW RTTY, three (KC2DAC, MM9PSY, MM0ZBH) were in fact on the air spotting CW and published zero RTTY; only K3FEF, M9PSY-2 and KW4VA were absent entirely. An earlier draft of this report called all six "not on air", which was wrong and understated the case.
  • K1RA is the clearest evidence of a change, measured at 5.4 percent on ~32,000 judged spots in CQ WW and 45–48 percent two weeks later. The change cannot be dated more precisely: RTTY is contest-driven, there is almost none between those weekends, and Makrothen is the next RTTY contest after CQ WW, so there is no intermediate weekend to bisect.
  • CQ WW RTTY was not free of this. VU2OY-9 reached 85.8 percent there, on 677 judged spots, with CN3A at 24.7 and OK4ZW at 21.2. What distinguishes Makrothen is not that a skimmer went wrong but that eight did at once, each on a large sample: at a 1,000-judged floor CQ WW's worst is 15.0 percent, 2.6× its median, while Makrothen still shows six skimmers at 43–50 percent.
  • The weekends differ in size — 1,427,716 RTTY spots against 195,068 — so only the per-skimmer comparison carries weight, never the aggregate difference between contests.
  • The common factor is established; the defect is not. Shared software explains which skimmers misbehave, and the measurements in sections 2–6 constrain what kind of fault it is: flat in pile-up size, spread across nearly every runner, correct callsigns at correct frequencies. What the defect actually is, and which version introduced it, needs someone with the source — this report is intended to hand them a reproducible target.
