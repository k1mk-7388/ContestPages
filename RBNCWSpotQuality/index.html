    Investigations into the Quality of CW Spots on the Reverse Beacon Network

    What Followed the Makrothen RTTY Defect

    Abstract

    The defect that made eight RBN skimmers report callers as runners during the 2026 Makrothen RTTY contest was traced to one skimmer program, UberSDR Skimmer, and was fixed in RTTY within three weeks. That investigation is complete and is documented separately in Investigation into an RBN Anomaly during the 2026 Makrothen RTTY Contest. The same skimmers also ran a smaller excess of off-consensus spots in CW, which the RTTY fix did not touch and which a CW fix released on October 27 has not reduced. Starting from that excess, this document assesses the quality of CW spots on the RBN by several measures, not one: how often a spot disagrees with the consensus of other skimmers, and why; how often it names the wrong station as the one calling CQ; how often another skimmer confirms it; how often a cluster's quality filter lets it through; and whether its signal report is a measurement at all. The largest part of the excess is callers misattributed as runners: from October 10 to November 6 the program's skimmers reported them on a median of 1.83 percent of their judged CW spots, against 0.76 percent for the reference skimmers.

    Measured on the same signal at one station, the program now matches VE3NEA's in RTTY and still reports nearly twice as many misattributed callers in CW (§ 2). A second defect, also confined to CW, has frozen the signal reports of re-spots since about October 3 (§ 5). A confirmed rate, the usual check on a skimmer's quality, cannot see misattributed callers (§ 3); a cluster's quality filter stops most of them at a contest's peak, but not those vouched for by other copies of the same program (§ 4); and Appendix C measures what a busy CW band produces with no defect at all. Unlike the RTTY defect, these findings are still changing as the program is updated, and this document is revised as new RBN archives are published.

    Introduction

    This document continues an investigation that began with a bandmap that looked wrong during the 2026 Makrothen RTTY contest. The RTTY defect it uncovered has been resolved, and the document on it is now a closed record (the Makrothen document). What it found in CW has not been resolved, and is followed here. Despite uninformed speculation to the contrary, the misattributed-caller problem has been recognized and brought under control in RTTY; it is in CW that a residual problem with misattributed callers, also called "ghost CQ" spots, continues to show itself.

    The method and definitions are those of the Makrothen document and are not repeated here: spots are judged against the consensus of other skimmers as in Makrothen § 2; the UberSDR Skimmer roster and the reference skimmers are defined in its introduction; and its terminology is used throughout. The reference skimmers, also called the controls, are the seven comparison skimmers of Makrothen Table 1: AA4PA, HB9EMP, K9LC, OE9GHV, RN4WA, S50ARX, and SZ1A. Wherever this document compares with the controls it means these seven; comparisons with other skimmers or skimmers not on the roster say so. They were chosen for the RTTY comparison of that table, as established skimmers not running UberSDR Skimmer and active on the weekends compared, and they serve in CW because they meet what a CW control needs. Six of the seven show VE3NEA's program in the RBN's node list (OE9GHV is not listed). Their membership is fixed, so a day-by-day series compares the same skimmers throughout. And they are typical rather than flattering: from October 10 to November 6 their pooled misattributed-caller rate was 0.76 percent of judged CW spots, against a median of 0.71 (middle half 0.59 to 0.85) for the 186 skimmers not on the roster with at least 10,000 judged spots, and one by one they fall between the 13th and 84th percentiles of those 186. Five are in Europe and two in North America, while the roster is mostly in North America and Great Britain, so where that could matter, as in the North American Sweepstakes, a same-continent baseline is given as well (§ 1.3). References into that document are labeled Makrothen. Two of its sections apply here as much as there: a misattributed caller is a real station, so the cluster's CT1BOH quality flags pass it (Makrothen § 16), and the case for testing new software before it reaches a shared network (Makrothen § 17) is the same in CW.

    Where it matters which program a skimmer runs, the program is taken from the RBN's node list, which has shown UberSDR Skimmer by name since November 8, or from the frozen signal reports that only that program produces (§ 5.6). A skimmer that PSK Reporter lists as running the program may still send its CW spots from another: UberSDR's own "cwskimmer" add-on, for one, runs VE3NEA's Skimmer Server for CW (RBN-OPS #9636, #9663).

    The sections take the measures in turn. § 1 measures the CW excess of off-consensus spots: its size and makeup, what CW does not show, how much it disturbs the spot stream, the CW fix that has not shown up, the absence of any other identifiable group, and how caller spots rise with activity. § 2 compares the program with VE3NEA's on the same signal at one station. § 3 shows why a confirmed rate cannot see misattributed callers, § 4 measures how many a cluster's quality filter lets through, and § 5 follows the frozen signal reports; § 6 lists the sources. Appendix A gives the detail behind the CW excess; Appendix B sets it against the network's long-term CW record; Appendix C measures how many misattributed callers a busy CW band shows with no defect; and Appendix D holds the search for other groups of skimmers with an excess.

    1 · A Persistent Excess in CW

    The eight skimmers of the Makrothen investigation spot CW as well as RTTY, between 3246 and 14,298 CW spots each over the Makrothen weekend, so the test that found the RTTY defect can show whether they also behaved differently in CW, and they did: every one of the eight was elevated (Table 1), the excess appearing between the CQ WW and Makrothen weekends as it did in RTTY, while the controls remained unchanged.

    The CW excess is smaller, and when the RTTY defect was corrected the week of October 12 the CW excess stayed where it was (Makrothen § 15). The unchanged CW excess suggests that the RTTY correction did not address its cause. Separate causes are plausible, but the source code would be needed to establish that distinction. This section and Appendix A record what the CW spots show; none of the Makrothen document's RTTY findings depend on them. Appendix B sets the excess against 15 years of the network's CW record, in which it does not show at all.

    Every CW figure here excludes the international beacon frequencies. The NCDXF/IARU beacon project places 18 beacons on a single frequency per band (14100.0, 18110.0, 21150.0, 24930.0, and 28200.0 kHz), each transmitting for 10 seconds in rotation. Because a five-minute consensus slot can designate only one holder, my method classified every other beacon heard on that frequency in that slot as off-consensus, although each spot was entirely correct.

    The artifact could not simply be subtracted, because it accounted for 65.3 percent of K3FEF's off-consensus CW spots, 56.3 percent of MM0ZBH's, and 55.6 percent of K1RA-4's, but 0.0 percent for four of the seven controls, which do not monitor those frequencies. Left in, the beacon spots would inflate the CW excesses by a factor of roughly two to three. I discovered the artifact by examining a newly added skimmer's first 23 spots individually, which is a persuasive argument for inspecting individual spots and not only aggregate rates.

    The Makrothen RTTY analysis is unaffected, because the beacons transmit CW and I discard every non-RTTY spot before any grouping. Repeating Makrothen §§ 3–10 with these frequencies excluded leaves the group median at 44.6 percent, since 0.86 percent of all off-consensus RTTY spots fall on a beacon frequency, and at most 1.4 percent for any individual skimmer.

    1.1 · What CW Does Not Show

    Two findings that hold in RTTY do not hold in CW, and both are significant.

    • The software no longer singles out the worst skimmers. In RTTY the eight occupied the entire top of the RTTY ranking and no other skimmer exceeded 21.6 percent, but in CW over the Makrothen weekend they do not lead: OH2BBT at 52.1 percent, OK2RZ at 51.2, K5KHK at 34.0, and OK4YL at 30.5 are all worse, and none of them runs UberSDR Skimmer. Three of those four have receiver clock errors of the kind found at VU2OY-9 (Makrothen § 8): OH2BBT at −12.9 ppm, OK2RZ at +14.9, and OK4YL at +14.7. With each error removed, their rates decline to 4.6, 3.5, and 4.1 percent, close to the CW median. K5KHK has no clock error and stays essentially unchanged, at 34.1 percent; it was also 41.1 percent in CW over the CQ WW RTTY weekend. Even after correction, a small number of skimmers running other software, K5KHK among them, remain above the UberSDR group, which is elevated within the general population of skimmers rather than at its upper extreme (Figure 1); removing every skimmer's clock error leaves the group's CW median at 7.1 percent and the controls' at 3.8, so the CW excess is not attributable to frequency error.
    • The test of whether the calls are real stations stops discriminating. In RTTY, 90 to 97 percent of the distinct callsigns each of the eight reported off-consensus were spotted by four or more skimmers, the reporting skimmer itself included, during the Makrothen periods, against 80 to 94 percent for the seven reference skimmers of Makrothen Table 1, already a weak separation; Makrothen Table 4, which counts spots rather than callsigns and also accepts a call that held a frequency, separates them more (97 to 98 percent against 49 to 81). In CW both lie between 77 and 89 percent. The test therefore no longer distinguishes the groups. It establishes that most discrepant callsigns belong to real stations, but does not by itself identify the mechanism; Table 2 splits the CW off-consensus spots by mechanism instead.

    That CW was affected too might have helped locate the defect: the defect may not have been just a broken RTTY decoder; it could have been something common to both modes, such as the interpretation that decides which callsign holds the frequency. The disparity is much larger in RTTY, but these weekends alone are consistent with either a shared cause or separate causes. Makrothen § 15 adds evidence for operational separation: a fix that cleared RTTY left CW unchanged. Separate causes remain plausible, not established.

    1.2 · How Much Each Disturbed the Spot Stream

    How much a skimmer contributes to the stream's measured disagreement depends on two things: its off-consensus rate and how much of the stream it supplies. The RTTY excess was about 11 times larger, and the eight supplied about six times as much of the spot stream. Measured as excess off-consensus spots relative to each mode's judged traffic, the RTTY disturbance was about 65 times the CW one over the Makrothen weekend:

    • Size of the excess. In RTTY the eight ran 39.7 points above the controls (44.6 against 5.0 percent, 9.0 times; the excess is calculated from unrounded rates); in CW, 3.5 points (7.3 against 3.8 percent, 1.9 times).
    • Share of the stream. RTTY is thinly skimmed: the eight were 8 of 43 skimmers and sent 35.6 percent of all RTTY spots. In CW the same eight were 8 of 245 and sent 5.9 percent.
    • Corroboration around each spot. A decided RTTY cell had a median of 7 distinct skimmers, a CW cell 10, so an off-consensus CW spot typically has more consensus support against which to compare it.
    • Net effect. Beyond what the controls' rate would give, the eight sent about 17,800 excess off-consensus spots in RTTY, 13 percent of every judged RTTY spot, against about 1900 in CW, 0.2 percent of every judged CW spot. They supplied 83 percent of all off-consensus RTTY spots but 8 percent in CW, and left an off-consensus spot in 31 percent of decided RTTY cells against 4 percent in CW.

    The CW excess has persisted since the RTTY fix, and the UberSDR Skimmer roster's share of the CW stream has grown as more skimmers have taken up the program: over October 19–24, the 15 roster skimmers on the air sent 10.1 percent of all CW spots, and their excess was 0.3 percent of judged CW spots. It remains a small disturbance in a deep pool; the RTTY defect was a large one in a shallow pool. Makrothen Appendix C and Appendix B measure the same contrast against a decade or more of each mode.

    That contrast is itself another risk of testing on the live network. How far a defect skews the total traffic depends not only on the defect but on how many other skimmers dilute it, and nobody knows either number until the spots are already out. Here the affected group supplied 5.9 percent of the stream among 245 CW skimmers but 35.6 percent among 43 RTTY ones; those are output shares, not estimates of the share attributable to a defect.

    1.3 · A CW Fix That Has Not Shown Up

    The UberSDR Skimmer development team has since treated CW as a problem of its own. The program's public changelog, kept in the mirror since October 27, lists under release v2026.10.27-3, published that day at 1331Z: "callers answering a CW runner were sometimes spotted as calling CQ on the runner's frequency." That is the caller-as-runner defect in CW. This fix followed the RTTY fix by 10 days and is the strongest sign yet that the CW excess reflects the same kind of mistake in a separate part of the program.

    The spots do not show the fix taking hold. Split the way Makrothen Table 4 splits RTTY, into garbles, runners reported on the wrong frequency, callers, and unconfirmed calls, the UberSDR Skimmer roster's off-consensus CW spots are callers more than anything else, and the callers did not fall: across the 11 roster skimmers measured on both sides of the release, they made up 1.94 percent of judged spots from October 19 to 26 and 1.96 percent from October 28 to November 6, against 0.72 and 0.71 percent for the controls. Day by day, the roster's median CW excess averaged 2.4 points before the release and 2.6 after, while the controls held between 2.8 and 3.9 percent. Updates are installed by hand, so the spots cannot say whether the operators have the release or whether it changed less than its note suggests.

    With nothing changed across the release, Table 2 pools all 28 days from October 10 to November 6 for each skimmer's split. Against the controls' 3.37 percent off-consensus, 1.17 of it garbles, 1.30 runners elsewhere, 0.76 callers, and 0.13 unconfirmed, the 19 roster skimmers run 4.4 to 7.4 percent. Callers are the one part of that excess clearly above the controls on every skimmer, by 0.3 to 1.6 points; runners reported on the wrong frequency add up to 1.3 points, garbles up to 1.2, and unconfirmed calls up to 0.7. Frequencies changing hands do not explain the callers: a handover counts as a runner elsewhere, and the slow handovers that could leak into the caller column are at most about a fifth of caller spots, for the roster and the controls alike. Removing every one of them leaves the roster at 1.47 percent of judged spots against 0.61, still 2.4 times (Appendix C.5).

I measured the five complete weeks after Makrothen, October 14 to November 12, by the same mechanism split as Table 2. These are all CW spots in each hour, not a selection of CWT participants.     1.4 · No Other Identifiable Group

    Is UberSDR Skimmer the only group of skimmers with a CW excess? Joining PSK Reporter's software table to each skimmer's CW rate, the test that found it (Makrothen § 10), finds no other: over November 1 to 7, the 26 skimmers running UberSDR Skimmer ran a median of 6.57 percent off-consensus, with callers at 2.17 percent, against 3.37 and 0.66 percent for the 211 others with at least 1000 judged spots, and no other program differed from the rest by more than about half a point. The public records cannot tell most programs apart, however: most skimmers do not report to PSK Reporter at all, and the RBN's own node list showed nearly every skimmer on November 9, UberSDR Skimmer's included, as VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server, so the absence of another identifiable group is largely the absence of the means to identify one. The two settings the node list does record do not explain the callers either. Skimmers set to send CQ spots only report misattributed callers at nearly the same rate as those sending all spots, 0.63 against 0.73 percent: a misattributed caller goes out already labeled CQ, so neither that setting nor the Aggregator, which on HF forwards only CQ spots and beacons, can stop it. The other setting, the Validation Level, shows up in garbles instead.

    Skimmers in Asia do run high, a median off-consensus rate of 9.5 percent against 3.2 in Europe and 3.5 in North America, but that is an artifact of propagation combined with frequency reuse, not a defect: an Asian skimmer correctly hears an Asian station on a frequency that the far more numerous European and North American skimmers give to a station of their own. Appendix D gives the detail of all three findings.

    1.5 · Caller Spots Rise with Activity

    Misattributed callers are a fact of life on a busy band even without any software defect. In the first 10 hours of the 2026 ARRL November Sweepstakes CW, a bandmap with a 10-minute spot timeout, fed only by North American skimmers outside the UberSDR Skimmer roster, would typically have shown 15 to 21 misattributed callers for every 100 runners on 40, 20, and 15 m; on the quiet days of November 3 to 6 it would have shown almost none. A loud runner had such a caller in about one five-minute slot in eight at the peak, against about one in 14 on the quiet days. The roster's skimmers, about 8 percent of CW skimmers, added nearly as many again. Seeing more caller spots therefore mostly measures activity: only a skimmer group's rate, measured against controls on the same frequencies and times (Makrothen § 2), can show a defect (Appendix C). Nor is setting skimmers to send CQ spots only a remedy: a misattributed caller already carries the CQ label (§ 1.4), and the cluster's CT1BOH quality flags pass it as both valid and CQ (Makrothen § 16).

    The confirmed rate answers the first of the two questions in Makrothen § 2, not the second. A misattributed caller is a real station on the air near that frequency, and between 73 and 89 percent of the spots this document classes as misattributed callers pass the confirmed test. What confirms them is rarely the caller itself: a station searching and pouncing calls, works the runner, and moves on, and that day the caller was genuinely running nearby for only 3 to 20 percent of them. In most cases the confirming report is another skimmer's misattribution of the same caller, which the innocent causes of C.3 and C.4 produce at several skimmers at once, since what one skimmer decodes from the air its neighbors decode too. A check for callers that looks only at the unconfirmed spots, as the report's does, sees only the remainder: run the report's way on that day, it finds 165 callers spotted as CQ for K3FEF and 31 for KM3T-2, where this document's method finds 218 and 44. So K3FEF, whose misattributed callers ran 2.33 percent of its judged spots, four and a half times W1NT-6's, scores 96.9 percent confirmed, within two points of every skimmer in the table.

    Part of that is correlated errors. A confirmation from another skimmer running the same program is not independent: copies of one program share the same defect, so their agreement is a common-mode error, not a check. On November 15, 44 percent of KM3T-2's confirmed misattributed callers and 42 percent of K3FEF's were confirmed only by other skimmers on the roster; none of W1NT-6's were. As the program spreads, its own copies confirm more of its errors, and a node's confirmed rate can rise with nothing improved. Bob N6TV made the same point on RBN-OPS on November 15: if several skimmers running one program make the same mistakes, clusters will count their confirmations as validations (RBN-OPS #9652). If this were humans and not computer programs, it would be confirmation bias, or looking for your keys under the streetlamp.

    The cluster's quality flags make the same kind of check. AR-Cluster's CT1BOH SKIMVALID flag marks a spot valid when two more spots of the same call appear near the same frequency (Makrothen § 16), and copies of one program can supply them for one another, so a correlated error can pass it; § 4 measures how often. Errors common to every copy of a program are exactly what testing a new program before it reaches a shared network is meant to catch (Makrothen § 17).

    The confirmed rate is a sound check that a callsign is real and was heard; it catches busted calls, and KM3T-2 does well on it and on this document's measure alike. As the only measure of a skimmer's quality, it cannot see the misattributed callers of § 1. Nor can one node speak for all instances of the program it runs: KM3T-2 is among the cleanest CW skimmers on the roster, at 4.4 percent off-consensus over the WAE DX RTTY weekend against a roster median of 7.6 (§ 1.3).

    4 · What the Cluster's Filter Lets Through

    Most operators do not take raw RBN spots. A retail cluster passes them through a quality filter, and the most widely used, AR-Cluster's CT1BOH SKIMVALID flag, decides from the cluster's own cache of recent reports (Makrothen § 16). On RBN-OPS after the Sweepstakes it was argued that most misattributed callers never pass such a filter, and that its threshold could simply be raised as the network grows (RBN-OPS #9581, #9597). The spots can measure both.

    AR-Cluster 6.6 publishes the rule in detail, with live traces of its decisions ("Inside SKIMVALID"). An incoming report is marked Valid when the cache holds two earlier reports of the same call in its neighborhood: the report's own 100 Hz bin and three bins on either side, among reports less than 27 minutes old. It counts reports, not distinct receivers, and an earlier report counts whatever its own classification was; a nearby, similar call already marked Valid can instead get the report marked Busted, which matters for garbles but not for a caller whose call is unlike the runner's. I applied that rule to the RBN archive, which carries the same spots as the feed a cluster receives, together with two variants: requiring three or five distinct receivers other than the sender, the raised thresholds proposed in its place.     It cannot stop correlated errors. Of the roster's misattributed-caller spots marked Valid, 40 to 45 percent would have failed without the reports of other skimmers on the roster: copies of one program vouch for one another (§ 3). Requiring five distinct receivers other than the sender cuts the misattributed-caller spots marked Valid at the Sweepstakes peak by about half, to 18.2 percent from the other skimmers and 9.9 from the roster, and holds back 4.0 and 6.1 percent of runner spots; on the quiet days it holds back 9.5 and 13.0 percent of runner spots while still passing 41.2 and 33.8 percent of misattributed callers. A newly heard runner's first spots also wait for the fifth receiver.

    So the cluster's filter answers the operator's question far better than a confirmed rate does (§ 3), but not completely: at a contest's peak a third of the other skimmers' misattributed-caller spots and a fifth of the roster's still reach a filtered bandmap, and close to half of the roster's that do are vouched for by its own copies.

    5 · Frozen Signal Reports

    A spot's signal report is a quality measure too, and since about October 3 a second defect, confined to CW, has emptied it of meaning on many re-spots: they repeat a stored signal-to-noise ratio in place of a new measurement. This section gives the report that brought it to light, the method, its history and scope, the releases, and how it is tracked.

    5.1 · The Report

    On November 15, Karel OK1CF asked on the Topband reflector whether others had noticed that K1HTV-4, KM3T-1, and K3GMQ had reported his signal at a constant strength for several days. The archive for November 16 shows him on 1825.8 kHz exactly as he described:

    • K1HTV-4: 41 dB on seven successive spots from 0027 to 0140Z, then 5 dB at 0215Z;
    • KM3T-1: 23, 10, and 18 dB from 0003 to 0025Z, then 60 dB on eight successive spots from 0035 to 0147Z, then 11 dB at 0211Z;
    • K3GMQ: 25 dB on five successive spots from 0015 to 0100Z.

    In the same hours KM3T-2 and W1NT-6, also reporting him, gave a different value on almost every spot. The held value need not be the first measurement: KM3T-1 measured three different values before settling on one it then repeated.

    5.2 · Method

    1. Data. Every CW spot in the RBN daily archive, beacons included, for the dates measured.
    2. Series. Group the spots by skimmer, station spotted, and band, and sort each group by time.
    3. Runs. Split each series into runs: a run ends when the next spot comes more than 15 minutes after the last, or lies more than 0.5 kHz from the run's first spot. A skimmer re-reports a station it is still hearing about every 10 minutes, so a run is one stretch of continuous hearing.
    4. Long runs. Keep runs of eight or more spots, an hour or more of hearing at the usual re-spot interval.
    5. Frozen runs. A long run is frozen when every spot in it carries the same signal-to-noise ratio.
    6. Rate. Each day, divide the frozen long runs by all long runs, separately for the roster's skimmers and for all other skimmers.

    Signal reports are whole decibels, so a steady station can repeat a value on a few successive spots by chance, but over eight spots and an hour or more, with propagation changing, chance agreement is rare: among skimmers not on the roster, 0 to 0.24 percent of long runs were frozen on any day from October 1 to November 17. For the history in 5.3 the roster is taken as listed by November 17 and applied to every day, since the record of PSK Reporter's software table begins on October 14; skimmers added later, some of them running VE3NEA's program, contribute long runs but no frozen ones, so the roster's rate is if anything understated.

    The defect is confined to CW. Over WAE DX RTTY, of 6138 long RTTY runs by roster skimmers none was frozen, and of 5059 by other skimmers one was. It affects only the signal report: the callsign and frequency of each re-spot are unaffected, so it adds no off-consensus spots and does not touch the rates elsewhere in this document. It does make a roster skimmer's signal reports unreliable after the first spot of each run, for anyone using them to compare receiving stations or follow propagation; such comparisons should use only the first spot of each run.

    An issue titled "Overflow error?" was opened on the program's public mirror at 0235Z on November 16, citing OK1CF's report, and was closed at 0654Z on November 17 with the comment "This should be fixed in latest." The latest release then was v2026.11.16-3, published at 2036Z on November 16 with the note "CW Skimmer precision improvements."     5.6 · A Fingerprint for the Program

    Because other skimmers almost never freeze a run, a skimmer that does is very likely running UberSDR Skimmer, whatever version string it reports. On November 16, six skimmers whose node-list entry gave VE3NEA's version string, v.1.6.0.145, had frozen runs: N6WAI, EI4HQ, EA8/DF4UE, MM0ZBH, K1HTV-4, and NB3A. These are most likely running releases from before the program began naming itself on November 8 (Appendix D.2), and N6WAI's entry showed v2026.11.16-3 by November 18. Conversely, KM3T-3 and WT8P, listed by PSK Reporter as running the program but reporting v.1.6.0.145, had many long runs and none frozen, consistent with their CW spots coming from VE3NEA's program. Absence on a single day is weaker evidence than presence.

    5.7 · Tracking

    The measurement is repeated as each day's archive is published, at about 0002Z the following day. Each run records the day's frozen share for the roster and for other skimmers, lists every roster skimmer with a frozen run alongside the release the node list shows for it, and names those with ten or more long runs and none frozen. It declares the defect fixed when the roster's share falls to 0.5 percent or less, about twice the highest daily share among other skimmers.

    Appendix A · The CW Excess in Detail

    This appendix holds the CW checks behind § 1. The CW excess persisted after the RTTY correction; whether the two have separate causes remains unconfirmed. None of the Makrothen document's RTTY findings depend on what follows. Appendix B sets the CW excess against the network's long-term CW record.

    A.1 · Not an Artifact of the Frequency Bin

    CW callers frequently transmit a few hundred hertz off frequency in order to be heard, which could place a caller outside the runner's own 100 Hz bin and conceal it from this test, the obvious explanation for why a genuine CW problem might appear mild. Repeating the analysis with tolerances of ±200 and ±500 Hz excludes that possibility: widening the bin raises every skimmer's rate, because it also combines genuinely separate stations, but it reduces the disparity rather than enlarging it, the group-to-control ratio falling from 1.9 to 1.7 to 1.4. There are no concealed offset callers to discover, and the narrow bin is the more discriminating measurement; on the CQ WW weekend the ratio is 1.1, 1.0, and 0.9 at the three widths, with no excess at any of them.

    A.2 · Not a Matter of Contest Load

    The weekend after Makrothen tested whether the CW excess grows with the traffic. WAG (Worked All Germany), on 80, 40, 20, 15, and 10 m, and the Stew Perry Topband Challenge, on 160 m only, both ran from 1500Z on October 17 to 1500Z on the 18th, in the middle of JARL WW RTTY. I compared that window with the same 24 hours, 1500Z to 1500Z, on eight quieter days from October 13 to 21, judging each contest on its own bands and setting the skimmers on the UberSDR Skimmer roster against the seven reference skimmers:

    • WAG's bands: no change. With about six times a quiet day's judged traffic (163,169 of the roster's spots, against 20,759 to 33,193), the roster's median was 6.3 percent against 3.1 for the controls, an excess of 3.2 points (2.0 times), inside the quiet days' range of 2.4 to 4.0 points (1.5 to 2.1 times). As in RTTY (Makrothen § 5), a busier band does not make the defect worse. Sweepstakes, whose exchanges begin with the caller's callsign, did raise the caller share (§ 1.3).
    • The Stew Perry, on 160 m: almost none. The roster's median was 3.0 percent against 2.7 (1.1 times), on 11,948 of its judged spots, far below its excess on the other bands. On quiet days 160 m carries too few decided cells to compare, and the spots do not show why the band behaves differently.

    Appendix B · The CW Record: No Comparable Break

    The measurement of Makrothen Appendix C, applied to three benchmark CW contests, ARRL DX CW (third full weekend of February), CQ WPX CW (last full weekend of May), and CQ WW CW (last full weekend of November), gives the comparison the RTTY record needs (Table 11 and Figure 4). The archive reaches back to 2009 for CW, but through 2010 there were too few skimmers and too little could be judged, so the series starts in 2011; by then there were 75 to 96 CW skimmers in each contest, and there are some 240 now.

    CW shows no trend of the kind RTTY does. A single least-squares line through all three contests falls by 0.018 points a year for callers and 0.023 for all off-consensus spots, against 0.23 and 0.18 for RTTY, so CW has been essentially flat. The median skimmer was off-consensus on 5.2 percent of judged spots across 2011 to 2020 (the median of those contests) and 4.4 percent across 2022 to 2025. Callers reported as runners were never a large part of CW's errors: a median of 1.0 percent of judged spots over the whole period, 0.83 to 1.12 percent from 2022 to 2025, with no trend. CW skimmers appear to have told callers from runners reliably all along, while RTTY skimmers took a decade to learn to do so.

    Underneath the flat total the mix changed much as it did in RTTY. Runners reported on the wrong frequency fell from a median of 3.0 percent of judged spots over 2016–2020 to 1.9 over 2023–2025, and garbles rose from 1.2 to 2.0; garbles roughly doubled in RTTY over the same years. Calibration improved even more than in RTTY: the median skimmer's clock error, 1.0 to 2.1 ppm from 2011 to 2020, has been 0.4 to 0.7 ppm since 2023, and the clock correction that once lowered a contest's network rate by up to 2.5 points now lowers it by at most 1.0.

    Measured the same way, the Makrothen weekend's CW (both UTC days, as in § 1) was on trend: the network was off-consensus on 4.85 percent of judged spots, within the 4.4 to 5.3 percent of the benchmarks of 2022 to 2025. Its caller share, 1.38 percent, sat a little above the benchmarks' 0.83 to 1.12 percent, but was still 1.32 percent without the eight, so the eight are not the reason.

    However large or small the increase in off-consensus spots at the UberSDR Skimmer instances, in CW its effect on the network's statistics is diluted to the point of invisibility. The CW excess of § 1 is real skimmer by skimmer, but over the Makrothen weekend the eight were 8 of some 245 CW skimmers and sent 5.9 percent of the CW spots (§ 1.2): leaving them out moves the network's off-consensus rate by 0.15 points, from 4.85 to 4.70 percent, and its caller share by 0.06, from 1.38 to 1.32, well inside the scatter between one benchmark contest and the next. In RTTY the same eight were 8 of 43 skimmers and sent a third of the spots, and leaving them out moves the network rate by 13.7 points (calculated from unrounded rates), from 18.4 to 4.6 percent, and the caller share by 9.3, from 10.5 to 1.1. It is the same contrast § 1.2 draws, a small disturbance in a deep pool and a large one in a shallow pool, now set against a decade or more of each.

    That makes the RTTY record the sharper: in CW, callers spotted as runners were always rare and stayed rare; in RTTY they had become rare, and at Makrothen were suddenly more than 10 times the norm (Makrothen Appendix C).

    Appendix C · Misattributed Callers on a Busy CW Band

    How many misattributed callers does a busy band produce with no software defect involved, and how does that number depend on activity? Anecdotal evidence cannot answer it: an operator who notices more caller spots on the bandmap may be seeing a defect or may only be seeing a busy contest. To measure it I took the first 10 hours of the 2026 ARRL November Sweepstakes CW, from 2100Z November 7 to 0700Z November 8, after which run rates fall off sharply, and the quiet days of November 3 to 6 for comparison. The 10-hour window spans two UTC days, November 7 and 8; the consensus for each of those two days, which decides who holds each frequency and whether a call was heard elsewhere that day, is built from all of that day's spots, but only cells inside the 10-hour window are counted, as in § 1.3. The quiet days are taken whole. A caller is defined as in Table 2; beacon frequencies are excluded and clock errors are not removed. Skimmers on the UberSDR Skimmer roster (the roster below) are counted separately from all others, which are overwhelmingly VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server (Appendix D.2).

    C.1 · By How Loud the Runner Is

    A runner's loudness is the number of distinct skimmers backing its decided cell, a five-minute slot on one 100 Hz frequency. For a loud runner, one backed by 30 to 79 skimmers, at least one skimmer outside the roster reported a misattributed caller in 12.5 to 12.9 percent of its slots during the Sweepstakes peak, against 6.5 to 7.0 percent on the quiet days (Table 12). With 12 slots an hour, that is about 1.5 slots an hour of running at the peak, against about 0.8 on the quiet days. The share stops rising once about 20 skimmers back the runner, because the caller rate per spot falls as the runner gets louder, from 1.66 percent to 0.24: the extra skimmers mostly report the runner itself, and the number of slots with a caller spot does not grow with them. A model that treats each skimmer's 0.66 percent median caller rate (Appendix D.1) as an independent chance per spot therefore overstates a loud runner's share by roughly two to four times. The roster's skimmers, about 8 percent of CW skimmers, put a caller into 10.9 to 11.6 percent of a loud runner's slots at the peak, nearly as often as all the other skimmers together and two to three times their own quiet-day share.

    C.3 · An Innocent Slip, Faithfully Reported

    "For our policemen, we created a race of robots. … we have given them absolute power over us. This power cannot be revoked."

    Klaatu, The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951)

    A skimmer has to decide from the decoded text alone which callsign is calling CQ. The simplest workable rule is that the call right after a CQ or TEST is the station calling CQ, and that a CQ belongs only to the first call after it, so a caller answering on the runner's own frequency is passed over. When a caller answers zero beat, the decoder hears both stations as one stream of text:

    Runner K1MKCQ SS K1MK Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    Read as "CQ SS K1MK W0XYZ", the CQ goes to K1MK, the first call after it, and W0XYZ is passed over as a caller. OpenSkimmer, Karl K5KHK's open-source skimmer (MIT license) and one of two CW skimmers whose interpretation code is public, the other being SparkGap (below), uses that rule with two guards: a TEST right after a call the skimmer already knows on that signal belongs to that call, and on a frequency it already credits to one station a new call needs its CQ immediately in front of it (callsign.py). VE3NEA has not published Skimmer Server's rules; this one serves here as a plain, reasonable baseline.

    Hypothesis, requiring confirmation: two ordinary operating slips, with no defect anywhere, can defeat that rule. A caller who presses the key for the CQ message (F1 in N1MM's default layout) instead of the one for their own call (F4), and stops it a moment too late, puts a stray CQ on the runner's frequency just ahead of their call:

    Runner K1MKCQ SS K1MK Caller W0XYZCQ (stopped) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    A runner whose automatic CQ repeat starts just as a caller answers, and who stops it, leaves the same kind of fragment, either a CQ after its own call or a CQ cut off before it:

    Runner K1MKTU K1MK CQ (stopped) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ
    Runner K1MKCQ SS (stopped) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    Nor need the slip be the misattributed caller's own. A stray CQ from a second caller on the frequency credits whichever call is decoded next, so an innocent third caller can be spotted as calling CQ for another station's mistake:

    Runner K1MKCQ SS K1MK Second callerCQ (stopped) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    Nor need that second station be zero beat. A skimmer copies each signal in a narrow channel, about 47 Hz wide in OpenSkimmer, and a station calling a little off frequency, or loud enough for its signal to bleed into the channel copying the runner, lands in the same stream of text. Its stray CQ then sits in front of whichever call that channel decodes next.

    In every one of these cases the rule does exactly what it says. The word in front of W0XYZ is a CQ, with no other call between them, so W0XYZ is credited with calling CQ. Neither guard applies: the first covers a TEST, not a CQ, and the second is satisfied because nothing at all stands between the CQ and the call. Once given, the credit stays with that call, so its next clean copy, such as the caller's own call in the Sweepstakes exchange, completes the spot. And because the stray CQ was transmitted, every skimmer applying such a rule to that frequency reads the same text: several can report the same misattributed caller at the same moment, however strong or weak the signals.

    A third case needs no slip at all. A runner usually sends its call only once after the CQ. If noise, a fade, or another station's signal turns that call into something that is not a callsign, the first caller decoded cleanly after it becomes the first call after the CQ:

    Runner K1MKCQ SS KJMK (K1MK, its 1 decoded as J, the final dah lost) Caller W0XYZW0XYZ

    The two open-source CW skimmers handle this differently: OpenSkimmer, and SparkGap, a multiband Linux skimmer for CW, RTTY, and FT8 (GPL-3.0 license) in daily use at WF8Z, whose spotting rules are likewise public. In OpenSkimmer a garbled word that is neither a callsign nor an unreadable "?" does not stop the search for the call after the CQ, so W0XYZ is credited with calling CQ unless that skimmer has already established K1MK on the signal, by spotting it or copying it cleanly twice; once credited, the caller's next clean copy completes the spot. SparkGap names W0XYZ too but is unlikely to spot it (spot_rule.py). Its exchange test, which treats a call followed by a report and a number as a caller being sent the exchange, would catch the case in a contest such as CQ WW but not in the Sweepstakes, whose exchange has no report and begins with a serial number. What protects the Sweepstakes example is the rest: while K1MK owns the frequency, a CQ naming another call in the same decode window does not count, and the runner's own call is in that window whenever its exchange is, since the Sweepstakes exchange includes it; and a call needs two to four separate decode windows within 10 minutes, so one such event is not enough. Its guard against a garbled runner's call, which would block the case outright, is off by default.

    If this case is common, a skimmer should misattribute callers mostly when it is not copying the runner. Table 14 tests that with each skimmer's own spots: for every misattributed caller in the samples of C.1, it asks whether the same skimmer had itself spotted the runner, within 300 Hz, in the 10 minutes before, only in the 10 minutes after, or in neither. VE3NEA's Skimmer Server spots a station at most once in 10 minutes, so a skimmer copying a runner that keeps running would spot it at least once in that 20-minute span; no spot of the runner there means the skimmer was not copying the runner's call.

    A public case may be one of these. The archive cannot show whether a CQ leaked, or from whom.

    Nothing in that chain is a defect. There was "Rotten QRM"1 (as there has been for nearly 110 years; C.4 measures it), or an operator made a slip every contester makes, or the decoder misread one word, as every decoder sometimes will, since none decodes without error; the rule was then applied as written, and the Aggregator forwarded a spot labeled CQ, as it should. No rule that reads text alone can tell a leaked CQ from a real one: a CQ message that goes out whole ("CQ SS W0XYZ") is, in the text, exactly what it says. And once the spot is sent, nothing recalls it. The RBN has no way to withdraw a spot, and a bandmap keeps it for its whole timeout. That is the bargain the network makes: judgment is handed to machines that apply a rule faithfully, in milliseconds, and cannot be overruled afterward. Nearly always that is exactly right. Here a burst of QRM, a slipped key, or one misread word, each lasting a fraction of a second, becomes a published spot that no one can take back.

    A lot of stuff happens during a contest. A busy run frequency is a mildly chaotic place: keys slip, macros fire and are cut off, and several callers pile onto one frequency at once. Seen against that, it is remarkable that skimmers reading nothing but decoded text, by rules as plain and simple as these, keep misattributed callers to fewer than 1 in 100 judged spots: a median of 0.66 percent for skimmers not running UberSDR Skimmer (Appendix D.1).

    The RBN archive records spots, not the decoded text behind them, so it cannot show how often this happens; recordings of a contest band, replayed through a skimmer that logs its decoded text, could. The replay in § 2 compared spots, not that text. The rule is OpenSkimmer's, which decodes CW only, and it suits CW, where a runner's call nearly always follows its CQ or TEST. It would fail in RTTY, where practice varies among those calling CQ and a runner's call often comes before the keyword as well as after it ("TEST K1MK K1MK CQ"): a rule that gives a CQ to the first call after it would hand that closing CQ to the next caller. RTTY skimmers rely instead on per-contest descriptions of how runners call CQ, such as VE3NEA's Contests.ini and the --rtty-contests file UberSDR Skimmer added after Makrothen (Makrothen § 14).     C.5 · Recycled Frequencies

    Run frequencies are recycled: a runner moves on and another starts on the same frequency, sometimes within one five-minute cell. The judging method does not follow a handover, but it is built not to mistake one for a misattributed caller. Each cell is judged on its own: its holder is the call reported by the most distinct skimmers in that slot (the one spotted first, on a tie), and the cell is decided when at least four back it. When a frequency changes hands within a slot, only one runner can hold the cell, and every spot of the other is off-consensus, part of every skimmer's rate, the controls' included (Makrothen § 2). The mechanism split then tests such a spot before the caller test: a call that holds any decided cell in the same slot or the one before or after is a runner elsewhere (Table 2). The outgoing runner normally held the cell in the slot before, and the incoming one holds it in the slot after. A handover, a late report of a runner that has just left, and a runner that moves a few kilohertz all land there, not among the callers.

    A handover reaches the caller column only when it is slow: when the runner that lost the cell holds no decided cell within one slot either side, as on a quiet band or in a short run backed by fewer than four skimmers, and the call still passes the caller test, having held a decided cell at some other time that day or been reported by four skimmers. To bound that leak I counted the caller spots whose call held the same frequency, on the same band and within one 100 Hz bin, two or three slots away (10 to 15 minutes) and, more generously, up to six (30 minutes). From October 10 to November 6 these were 16 and 20 percent of the controls' caller spots and 17 and 21 percent of the roster's, 14 to 31 percent skimmer by skimmer. Not all are handovers, since a station that answered a runner and later ran on the same frequency qualifies too, so these are upper bounds. Removing every one of them lowers the roster's caller rate from 1.86 percent of judged spots to 1.47 and the controls' from 0.76 to 0.61; the ratio between them stays at 2.4. Slow handovers can inflate both groups' caller counts by about the same fraction, and they do not explain the roster's excess.

    Appendix D · Looking for Additional Groups

    This appendix holds the detail behind § 1.4: the search for additional groups of skimmers with a CW excess, what the RBN's node list can and cannot say about the software and settings behind each skimmer, and the regional excess that turned out to be propagation.

    D.1 · Software Groups

    Is UberSDR Skimmer the only group of skimmers with a CW excess? The test is the one that found it (Makrothen § 10): join PSK Reporter's software table to each skimmer's CW rate. Over November 1 to 7, the seven days that table describes, 237 skimmers had at least 1000 judged CW spots, each with its receiver clock error removed (Makrothen § 9), and their median off-consensus rate was 3.41 percent. Of the 11 programs listed for at least three of them, only UberSDR Skimmer differs from the rest: its 26 skimmers ran a median of 6.57 percent against 3.37 for all the others, and callers at 2.17 percent against 0.66, a difference far past what testing 11 groups at once requires (Mann–Whitney p of 5 × 10−8, against a threshold of 0.0045). The UberSDR platform itself, whose operators skim CW with VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server, ran at 3.39 percent, and no other program differed from the rest by more than about half a point.

    That search is only as good as the software labels, and they are thin. Of the 237 skimmers, 137 do not report to PSK Reporter at all, and for many that do, the table names the bridge that forwards their spots, CW reporter, not the skimmer behind it. The RBN's own detailed node list, which records a version string for every connected skimmer, cannot fill the gap. Of the 259 nodes it listed on November 9, 217 reported skimmer version v.1.6.0.145, the version of VE3NEA's CW Skimmer Server, and so did every one of the 19 UberSDR Skimmer nodes on it: a program that attempts to duplicate Skimmer Server's interface reports Skimmer Server's version. (A UberSDR Skimmer release on November 8 changed this for nodes running that software; by November 12, 14 nodes reported UberSDR Skimmer and its release instead.) On November 9 only five nodes named their program at all (SparkGap 1.2 on three, Wave Owl 1.17.0 and Hammerhead 0.1.0 on one each); by November 12, with UberSDR Skimmer's 14, there were 19. The four Meridian stations stand out only because they send the same digits without the "v.". Other than UberSDR Skimmer, which reports itself to PSK Reporter by name, a group of skimmers sending Skimmer Server's version string cannot be told from Skimmer Server itself by any public record, so the absence of another identifiable group is largely the absence of the means to identify one.

    D.2 · What the RBN Node List Records

    The node list records two settings each skimmer runs with, both reported to a SKIMMER/SETT query: its Validation Level, which sets how strictly a decoded callsign must pass the program's checks (watch list, Master.dta, callsign pattern, ITU block) before it is spotted, from Minimal through Normal and Aggressive to Paranoid, with "the more callsigns are rejected" the higher the level (CW Skimmer manual), and a spot filter that sends either all spots or CQ spots only. How many clean decodes a callsign needs before it is spotted depends on the level and on whether the call is on the operator's watch list or in the program's pattern file of known callsign patterns (Table 16): at Normal, a call the pattern file does not know must be decoded five times, at Minimal twice, and at Aggressive it is never spotted. Joining the list of November 9 to the same CW rates (206 of the 237 skimmers appear on it) shows neither setting separating the callers (Tables 17 and 18). The filter makes no difference in any category; skimmers set to send CQ spots only report callers at nearly the same rate as the rest, 0.63 against 0.73 percent. A caller misattributed as a runner is still sent as a CQ spot and passes the filter, consistent with the defect lying in the skimmer's interpretation rather than its decoding. All 19 UberSDR Skimmer nodes report the same settings, Normal Validation Level and all spots; the 113 other skimmers with those settings share 0.69 percent callers, so the settings do not explain the UberSDR Skimmer roster's excess either. Validation Level shows up in decoding instead, as its purpose suggests: the 16 skimmers at Minimal, the most lenient level, run 4.17 percent off-consensus against 3.34 for the 168 at Normal, mostly garbles, 1.77 against 1.21 percent (Mann–Whitney p of 1 × 10−6), while their callers differ only marginally (p of 0.04, above the 0.01 that testing five mechanisms at once requires). The three at Aggressive, which rejects more callsigns, have the fewest garbles, 0.38 percent. Both fit Table 16: a misdecode that no known pattern matches needs two clean decodes at Minimal rather than five, and is never spotted at Aggressive. Whether the Minimal level itself admits the garbles, or something else those 16 stations share, the spots cannot say.

    D.3 · SparkGap and OpenSkimmer

    Two other skimming programs come into the search, and neither can be tested as a group: one is named by only three nodes, the other by none. SparkGap 1.2 is the program those three nodes name on the RBN node list (Appendix D.2); those three, two of them one operator's, ran a median of 6.0 percent off-consensus with callers at 1.5: suggestive, but too few to call a group.

    OpenSkimmer is an open-source CW skimmer that Karl K5KHK published on November 10. Karl's own node, K5KHK, stands out: § 1.1 finds it above the UberSDR Skimmer group in CW over the Makrothen weekend with no clock error. It appears on the RBN node list as Skimmer Server v.1.6.0.145 and is not in PSK Reporter's software table, so the public records do not name its program. Before September 2026 it scarcely appears in the archive: 4 CW spots in CQ WW CW 2024, none in the 2025 benchmark contests, and 28 on August 15. From October 10 to 18 it ran 16 to 50 percent off-consensus in CW, with unconfirmed calls at 5 to 11 percent of its judged spots; over October 19 to 22 it fell to about 5 percent, the network median, and has stayed there. Whether the node ran OpenSkimmer, the RBN archive cannot definitively say, but the node was very likely running OpenSkimmer in development.

    D.4 · An Excess Shared by Location, Not Program: Asia

    The 18 skimmers in Asia ran a median off-consensus rate of 9.5 percent and a median caller rate of 5.4 percent, against 3.2 and 0.6 percent for the 126 in Europe and 3.5 and 0.8 percent for the 80 in North America. That excess is simply an artifact of propagation combined with frequency reuse. More than half of their off-consensus spots name an Asian station on a frequency the consensus gives to a European or North American station, and in most of those cases no Asian skimmer heard that station: two stations on the same frequency at once, each heard only where its signal reaches, while the consensus is decided by the far more numerous skimmers of Europe and North America. The Asian skimmers report what they hear correctly; the method scores them against stations they cannot hear (Makrothen § 19).

    The 40 m band shows it most plainly. During daylight hours in Japan it is effectively a different band from 40 m in Europe and North America at the same hour, and vice versa. Over November 3 to 5 the Japanese skimmers were off-consensus on 48.5 percent of their judged 40 m spots between 0800 and 1700 local time (2300 to 0800Z), and 35.2 percent of those judged spots named an Asian station on a frequency held by a European or North American station that no Asian skimmer reported; during nighttime hours in Japan the same skimmers were off-consensus on 13.7 percent of their judged 40 m spots, and 8.1 percent of those judged spots were of that kind. On the higher bands, where all regions share the same propagation more often, the effect fades: on 20 m in daylight hours in Japan the figures were 7.8 percent off-consensus and 2.9 of that kind, and 15 m almost nothing. In daylight hours in Japan, 40 m alone accounts for 62 percent of all such spots.
