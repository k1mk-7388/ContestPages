How this was built

The same terminator-relative method the WW Digi plan used, retargeted to this contest's clock, bands and scoring:

  • An 84-day archive of spots to/from FN32 ± 1 grid square (148M spots, 24 Jun – 15 Sep 2026), reduced to opening and closing edges per CQ zone and band measured as offsets from the terminator that bounds each path, weighted by solar rotation (a spot counts exp(−age/27.28 d)), then resolved against the real sunrise and sunset of the contest weekend. That indirection is what lets a summer archive describe an equinox night.
  • 52,536 public CQ WW RTTY logs, 2009–2025 — 21.5 million QSO lines, both sides of every contact. This contest states its multipliers in the exchange (CQ zone from both stations, state or province from any W/VE end), so the multiplier census is read off the logs rather than inferred: 40 zones, 63 W/VE QTH multipliers and 312 countries, each with the bands and hours it actually appears on.
  • Operating sheets, one page per band station over the full 48 hours. Expected activity is contest entrants ever active that hour times how open the path is this weekend; a multiplier is chipped only in the hours reaching half of its own best hour, so a chip marks a good time to chase it rather than merely its existence.
  • Five great-circle animations, one per contest band, centred on FN32LL: bearing reads straight off the map, distance is radial, and a 30-minute window slides one minute per frame through the day.

What the season and the sun are doing

Two things separate this weekend from the August contest, and they pull in opposite directions.

The equinox helps. 26 September is four days past the September equinox: day and night are near-equal at every latitude, the terminator runs nearly north–south, and the solar declination is −1.1° against +9.2° in late August. Equinox conditions classically favour the high bands and trans-equatorial paths.

The flux and the field do not. SWPC's 27-day outlook — reissued 14 September with the weekend unchanged from the 7 September issue — puts it at F10.7 = 100–105 with Ap 18 and Kp 5 on Saturday, Ap 15 and Kp 4 on Sunday: a disturbed weekend at low flux, now called the same way two issues running rather than once. Measured flux has been very steady near 110 for a fortnight (mean 109, sd 4) against the sweep archive's 127 mean, so the openings drawn here are, if anything, generous on the high bands. A Kp-5 Saturday matters more in this contest than in most: polar and high-latitude zones are multipliers, and they are the first paths an auroral absorption event takes away. The auroral ovals on the animations are drawn at the nominal Kp≈2 extent, so read them as optimistic.

No historical weekend is a clean analogue. Contest-weekend flux over the log archive ran 67–87 in the solar-minimum years (2018–2021) and 141–196 near maximum (2022–2025). 2026 at ~105 falls in the gap — except for 2013, which ran at F10.7 104.5, within half a point of the forecast. If you want one year's logs to reason from, that is the one.

Reading the charts

The axis runs 0000Z to 0000Z — the contest clock, and a 48-hour contest repeats the same diurnal cycle twice, so one day is drawn. Scores under each zone are CQ WW's continent rule: 3 points off-continent, 2 within North America, 1 domestic. Multipliers are counted per band, which is why the per-band view is the one that matters: the same zone is a fresh multiplier on each of the five bands.

Caveat, stated on every chart: the offsets come from 24 Jun – 15 Sep 2026; the window drifts one way through the season, 15 h 17 m of daylight down to 12 h 29 m. The target night runs 11 h 58 m, 0 h 31 m shorter than the shortest day in it. That is why the edges are measured against the terminator rather than in UTC — only the offsets carry across that gap, and the Carrington weighting already leans the sample toward the window’s recent end. This is an extrapolation rather than an interpolation.
