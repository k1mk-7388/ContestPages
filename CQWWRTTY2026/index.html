How this was built

The same terminator-relative method the WW Digi plan used, retargeted to this contest's clock, bands and scoring:

  • An 83-day archive of spots to/from FN32 ± 1 grid square (133M spots, 02 Jul – 23 Sep 2026), reduced to opening and closing edges per CQ zone and band measured as offsets from the terminator that bounds each path, weighted by solar rotation (a spot counts exp(−age/27.28 d)), then resolved against the real sunrise and sunset of the contest weekend. That indirection is what lets a summer archive describe an equinox night.
  • 52,536 public CQ WW RTTY logs, 2009–2025 — 21.5 million QSO lines, both sides of every contact. This contest states its multipliers in the exchange (CQ zone from both stations, state or province from any W/VE end), so the multiplier census is read off the logs rather than inferred: 40 zones, 63 W/VE QTH multipliers and 312 countries, each with the bands and hours it actually appears on.
  • Operating sheets, one page per band station over the full 48 hours. Expected activity is contest entrants ever active that hour times how open the path is this weekend; a multiplier is chipped only in the hours reaching half of its own best hour, so a chip marks a good time to chase it rather than merely its existence.
  • Five great-circle animations, one per contest band, centered on FN32LL: bearing reads straight off the map, distance is radial, and a 30-minute window slides one minute per frame through the day.

What the season and the sun are doing

Two things separate this weekend from the August contest, and they pull in opposite directions.

Changed on 23 September: the mid-month flux trough has ended — 118 measured on the 22nd, above the outlook's weekend figure — so the warning posted here on the 21st that flux was slipping toward 98 no longer holds, and is corrected below. The 0030Z three-day forecast also now puts hard numbers on the pre-contest storm: it peaks Kp 5.33 (G1) early on the 24th and decays through the 25th, which is what the weekend call depends on.

The equinox helps. 26 September is four days past the September equinox: day and night are near-equal at every latitude, the terminator runs nearly north–south, and the solar declination is −1.1° against +9.2° in late August. Equinox conditions classically favor the high bands and trans-equatorial paths.

The field has relented; the flux has not. The 21 September outlook — the last one before the contest — moves the disturbance off the weekend and onto the days before it. The storm now peaks Thursday and Friday (Ap 20 / Kp 4 on the 24th, Ap 18 / Kp 5 on the 25th) and is decaying by the time we start: Ap 12 and Kp 3 on Saturday, Ap 8 and Kp 3 on Sunday. Two successive issues had put Kp 5 squarely on Saturday, so this is a real revision and not a rounding change. It is good news precisely where this contest is won — polar and high-latitude zones are multipliers, and they are the first paths auroral absorption takes away. It also means the Kp≈2 auroral ovals drawn on the animations are now close to right rather than optimistic, though a Friday storm can leave the high-latitude ionosphere unsettled into Saturday even as the index falls.

The three-day forecast now backs that shape with its own numbers rather than leaving it on the 27-day outlook alone. Issued 0030Z on the 23rd, it puts the peak at Kp 5.33 (G1) in the 03–06Z period on the 24th, still 4.67 at 06–09Z, and then nothing above 3.7 anywhere on the 25th. Two independent products agreeing that the disturbance lands on Thursday and is spent by Friday night is the strongest version of this call we will get before the 0000Z forecast on Saturday morning, which is the first one to cover the whole contest.

The flux dipped and has come back. It fell to a trough mid-month — 100, 97, 96, 97 through the 18th — and on the 21st this page warned that persistence pointed nearer 98 than the outlook's 105. That is no longer true: 101, 104, then 118 on the 22nd, the highest day since the 10th and above the outlook's weekend figure. Five-day mean 103 but with a standard deviation of 9 and a clear upward slope, so the useful statement is the direction, not the average — an active region is rotating into view rather than out of it. Treat 105 as a floor for the weekend rather than a ceiling.

That still sits below the sweep archive's 122 mean, so the openings drawn here remain somewhat generous on the high bands — but by less than last week, and 10m is now the band most likely to beat this chart rather than miss it. One day is one day: if the 118 does not hold into Friday, the earlier reading stands.

No historical weekend is a clean analogue. Contest-weekend flux over the log archive ran 67–87 in the solar-minimum years (2018–2021) and 141–196 near maximum (2022–2025). 2026 at ~105 falls in the gap — except for 2013, which ran at F10.7 104.5, within half a point of the forecast. If you want one year's logs to reason from, that is the one.

USNO publishes civil twilight only, so the nautical figures (sun 12° below the horizon) are computed here — they sit within a minute of the twilight bands shaded on the charts. Nautical rather than civil because that is the boundary the charts use: on an RTTY signal the band behaves as if it is night well before the sky finishes darkening.

Watch the clock change. The contest runs on UTC and starts at 0000Z Saturday, which is 8:00 pm EDT on Friday the 25th — the first night is already under way before the local calendar agrees it is Saturday. Both sunsets fall at 22:4xZ, roughly an hour and a quarter before the 0000Z day boundary, so each contest day opens with the low bands already in darkness.

Reading the charts

The axis runs 0000Z to 0000Z — the contest clock, and a 48-hour contest repeats the same diurnal cycle twice, so one day is drawn. Scores under each zone are CQ WW's continent rule: 3 points off-continent, 2 within North America, 1 domestic. Multipliers are counted per band, which is why the per-band view is the one that matters: the same zone is a fresh multiplier on each of the five bands.

Caveat, stated on every chart: the offsets come from 02 Jul – 23 Sep 2026; the window drifts one way through the season, 15 h 13 m of daylight down to 12 h 06 m. The target night runs 11 h 58 m, 0 h 09 m shorter than the shortest day in it. That is why the edges are measured against the terminator rather than in UTC — only the offsets carry across that gap, and the Carrington weighting already leans the sample toward the window’s recent end. This is an extrapolation rather than an interpolation.
